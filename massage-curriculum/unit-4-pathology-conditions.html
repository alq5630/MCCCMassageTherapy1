Block 3 · Medical Massage

Pathology Conditions & Massage Decisions

This lesson turns the conditions from Theory Exam #4 into a repeatable reasoning process: recognize the condition, identify what makes massage risky, choose useful modifications and know when symptoms require referral instead of treatment.

Don't memorize “massage: yes or no”

For every condition, ask: What tissue/system is involved? Is it acute or chronic? What symptoms are present today? Is there a red flag? Is the contraindication general or local? What pressure, position, movement or modality needs to change? That's much more useful than memorizing a blanket rule.

Fibromyalgia

What it is
A chronic condition characterized by widespread pain and tenderness, often with fatigue, sleep disturbance and heightened sensory sensitivity.
Main massage risk
Overtreatment. A session that feels tolerable in the moment can still contribute to increased soreness or a symptom flare afterward.
Potential benefits
Comfort, relaxation, improved body awareness and temporary reduction of muscular tension or perceived pain for some clients.
Modify
Start lighter and often shorter. Build pressure based on the client's history and response over time, not on an assumption that painful tissue needs deeper work.

Headaches: tension-type vs migraine

Tension-typeMigraine
Typical patternPressure/tightness, often bilateral, commonly associated with neck/scalp muscular tension.Often more intense and may be throbbing, unilateral or accompanied by nausea, light/sound sensitivity or aura.
Massage approachNeck, shoulders, suboccipitals, scalp, temporalis and other involved muscles may be useful when appropriate.During an attack, reduce sensory input. Dim/quiet environment and gentle work may be better tolerated, or the client may prefer to postpone.

Refer urgently for a sudden severe “worst headache,” new neurological deficits, headache after major trauma, or other symptoms suggesting a medical emergency.

Muscle strains

A strain is injury to muscle and/or tendon tissue. Acute strains can involve pain, swelling, bruising, weakness and protective guarding.

Acute phase
Don't strip deeply through freshly injured fibers or force painful stretching. Protect the area and work indirectly when appropriate.
Later healing
As tissue heals, progressively dosed massage and movement may help restore comfortable mobility. Match intensity to healing stage.
Chronic presentation
Address surrounding muscular tension, movement limitations and scar/tissue restrictions within training rather than simply attacking the painful point.
Refer
Severe loss of function, major bruising/swelling, suspected rupture or symptoms inconsistent with a minor strain need evaluation.

Spasms and cramps

A spasm is an involuntary muscle contraction and can range from brief twitching to sustained contraction. A cramp is typically a painful involuntary contraction. Gentle massage, comfortable stretching and relaxation may help uncomplicated muscular cramping.

Don't assume every calf cramp is muscular. New unilateral calf pain with swelling, warmth, redness or unexplained tenderness raises concern for a vascular problem such as DVT and shouldn't be massaged.

Osteoarthritis

Osteoarthritis is a degenerative joint condition involving cartilage and other joint tissues. Symptoms can include pain, stiffness, reduced ROM and activity-related discomfort.

Stable/baseline
Massage can address surrounding muscle tension and comfort. Gentle movement may be appropriate within pain-free range.
Acute irritation
Avoid aggressive direct work and forced end-range movement over a hot, swollen or highly painful joint.

Rheumatoid arthritis

Rheumatoid arthritis is a systemic autoimmune inflammatory disease, not simply “wear and tear.” During an active flare, joints may be painful, warm, swollen and vulnerable.

Massage decision: avoid aggressive work over actively inflamed joints. When disease is stable, massage elsewhere or gentle work may be appropriate based on symptoms, joint integrity, medications and medical status.

Tendinitis vs tendinosis

ConditionMeaningMassage reasoning
TendinitisMore acute inflammatory tendon presentation.Avoid aggressive friction or deep direct work over an acutely inflamed tendon.
Tendinosis / tendinopathyMore chronic degenerative or persistent tendon change.Appropriately selected friction, surrounding soft-tissue work and progressive loading/movement may be used when within training and tolerated.

The key exam distinction is stage. A technique that may be useful later in recovery can be irritating during an acute inflammatory presentation.

Plantar heel pain / plantar fasciitis

A common presentation is heel or plantar foot pain that is especially noticeable with the first steps after rest. Treatment shouldn't consist of digging into the most painful heel attachment.

Consider the entire lower-leg/foot system, including gastrocnemius, soleus and other tissues affecting ankle/foot mechanics. Avoid excessive direct pressure over acutely irritated tissue. Focal bony pain, major swelling or inability to bear weight warrants further evaluation.

Disc conditions and sciatica

Sciatica describes symptoms along the sciatic nerve distribution and may include radiating pain, tingling, numbness or weakness. It doesn't identify one single cause. Disc pathology is one possible cause among several.

Massage precaution
Don't aggressively compress or stretch in a way that reproduces or intensifies neurological symptoms. Track whether symptoms move farther down the limb or become more severe.
Emergency red flag
Back pain with new bowel/bladder dysfunction, saddle anesthesia or major/progressive weakness requires urgent medical evaluation.

Shin splints

Medial tibial stress syndrome is an overuse-related pain condition along the tibial region. Massage may address muscular contributors in the lower leg, but don't aggressively work directly over painful bone.

Important differential: highly focal bony tenderness, severe pain, pain at rest or worsening symptoms can raise concern for a stress fracture and should be evaluated rather than treated more deeply.

Carpal tunnel syndrome

Carpal tunnel syndrome involves compression of the median nerve at the wrist. Symptoms can include numbness, tingling, pain or weakness in the median-nerve distribution.

Address relevant forearm/hand muscular tension and mechanics within scope, but don't apply sustained heavy compression directly into the carpal tunnel when it reproduces neurological symptoms. Worsening weakness, persistent sensory loss or unclear neurological symptoms warrant referral.

Thoracic outlet syndrome

Thoracic outlet syndrome refers to compression/irritation of neurovascular structures traveling from the neck/chest toward the upper extremity. Symptoms can be neurological, vascular or mixed.

Massage may address relevant muscular contributors such as scalenes, pectoral tissues and shoulder-girdle tension when appropriate, but vascular signs change the situation. Marked color change, swelling, coolness or other concerning circulation changes call for caution and medical evaluation rather than aggressive compression.

Varicose veins

Varicose veins are enlarged, tortuous superficial veins associated with venous valve dysfunction. Avoid deep direct pressure over prominent varicosities because the vessels/tissues can be vulnerable and because a therapist can't determine clot status by palpation.

Massage elsewhere may still be appropriate. New unilateral swelling, warmth, redness or unexplained calf pain is a different presentation and should trigger DVT screening/referral reasoning.

Peripheral neuropathy

Peripheral neuropathy may cause numbness, tingling, burning, pain, weakness or altered proprioception. Diabetes is one cause, but there are many others.

The massage problem is unreliable feedback. A client may not accurately feel excessive pressure, heat or tissue irritation. Use conservative pressure and temperature, visually monitor tissue, and avoid assuming “they would've told me if it hurt.”

Raynaud syndrome

Raynaud syndrome involves episodic vasospasm, most commonly in fingers or toes, producing color changes and altered sensation. Cold is a common trigger.

Keep the client comfortably warm, avoid extreme temperature exposure and don't use aggressive techniques during an active episode. Persistent, severe or unusual vascular symptoms warrant medical evaluation.

Bursitis

A bursa is a small fluid-filled structure that reduces friction near joints. Bursitis is irritation/inflammation of a bursa. Acute bursitis may produce focal pain, tenderness and swelling.

Avoid deep direct pressure over an acutely inflamed bursa. Work surrounding tissues conservatively when appropriate and avoid provoking the painful movement pattern.

TMJ dysfunction

Temporomandibular disorders can involve jaw pain, clicking, limited opening, locking, headaches and muscular tension. Massage may address external muscles such as the masseter and temporalis and other cervical/shoulder contributors.

Intraoral work requires specific training, hygiene procedures, informed consent and compliance with applicable scope/rules. Don't force a locked jaw or treat unexplained acute facial swelling as ordinary muscle tension.

GERD

Gastroesophageal reflux disease involves reflux of stomach contents into the esophagus. Positioning matters. A client with active reflux may tolerate semireclined positioning better than lying flat, particularly after eating.

Avoid deep abdominal compression when it worsens symptoms. Chest pain shouldn't automatically be attributed to reflux by a massage therapist.

High-yield red flags across conditions

Stop, postpone or refer rather than treating through: new unilateral calf swelling/warmth/redness; sudden chest pain or unexplained shortness of breath; sudden neurological deficits; bowel/bladder changes with saddle anesthesia; rapidly progressive weakness/numbness; major unexplained swelling; fever/systemic illness; suspected fracture; uncontrolled bleeding; severe new pain with no clear benign explanation.

Theory Exam #4 pattern

The study guide emphasizes fibromyalgia, headaches, strains, spasms/cramps, osteoarthritis, tendinitis/tendinosis, plantar fasciitis, disc conditions/sciatica, shin splints, carpal tunnel syndrome, thoracic outlet syndrome, varicose veins, peripheral neuropathy and rheumatoid arthritis. For each one, know definition → risk → modification → useful treatment direction → referral trigger.

Quick clinical cases

  1. Fibromyalgia + delayed soreness after prior deep massage: shorten the session and reduce pressure rather than repeating the dose that caused the flare.
  2. Acute Achilles-area tendinitis: don't use vigorous cross-fiber friction over actively inflamed tissue.
  3. Chronic tendinopathy with no acute inflammation: appropriately dosed friction/soft-tissue work may be considered within training and tolerance.
  4. “Sciatica” plus increasing foot weakness: stop treating it as routine muscular tightness and recommend medical evaluation.
  5. Prominent varicose veins: avoid deep direct pressure over the veins rather than declaring the entire client contraindicated.
  6. Numb diabetic feet: pressure and thermal intensity should be conservative because protective sensation may be impaired.
  7. Hot swollen rheumatoid joint: avoid aggressive local work during the active flare.
  8. First-step morning heel pain: think plantar heel pain and address contributing lower-leg/foot tissues without attacking the painful attachment.
