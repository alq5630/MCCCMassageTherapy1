Block 3 · Medical Massage

Medical Massage & Clinical Decision-Making

Move from a general wellness routine toward condition-aware, goal-directed massage while staying firmly inside massage therapy scope. The core skill is deciding whether massage is appropriate, what needs to change, and when referral is the safest answer.

Medical massage isn't medical diagnosis

“Medical massage” describes massage adapted to a client's health status or functional goals. It doesn't give a massage therapist authority to diagnose disease, prescribe medication, independently treat medical emergencies, or promise a cure. The therapist gathers relevant history, recognizes precautions and red flags, performs massage-appropriate assessment, develops realistic goals, selects and modifies techniques, documents the response, and refers when the situation exceeds scope or competence.

The clinical reasoning sequence

1 · Ask
Chief concern, onset, location, quality, intensity, aggravating/relieving factors, prior episodes, medical evaluation, current treatment and client goals.
2 · Screen
Health conditions, medications, recent surgery/injury, infection, fever, vascular risk, neurological symptoms, pregnancy status, allergies and other contraindications.
3 · Assess within scope
Observe posture and movement; palpate tissue texture, temperature and tenderness; compare sides; use simple active/passive movement when appropriate. Describe findings rather than diagnosing pathology.
4 · Decide
Choose among normal massage, modified massage, local avoidance, medical clearance/referral, or postponing the session.
5 · Treat
Select positioning, pressure, duration, region and technique based on goals, risk and client response. More pressure isn't automatically more therapeutic.
6 · Reassess
Ask what changed, repeat relevant massage-appropriate observations, document objectively and use the response to guide future care.

Red flags: don't “work through” these

MBLEx-style questions often test whether the therapist recognizes when massage isn't the next step. Concerning findings include sudden chest pain or unexplained shortness of breath; signs consistent with a possible blood clot such as unexplained unilateral swelling, warmth, redness and pain; sudden weakness, facial droop, speech difficulty or other acute neurological change; severe unexplained pain; significant recent trauma; uncontrolled bleeding; high fever or systemic illness; rapidly spreading infection; and symptoms suggesting an acute medical emergency.

Exam principle: when a scenario suggests an emergency or condition outside massage scope, don't choose a clever massage modification. Choose the safest referral/emergency response.

Acute, subacute and chronic presentations

Acute
Recent onset and often more reactive. Screen carefully for injury and red flags. Aggressive pressure over acutely inflamed or injured tissue is generally inappropriate.
Subacute
Healing is underway but tissue may still be vulnerable. Treatment can progress gradually according to healing status, symptoms and medical restrictions.
Chronic
Long-standing doesn't automatically mean safe or “needs deep pressure.” Consider the underlying condition, medications, flare status, tissue tolerance and functional goals.
Flare vs baseline
A chronic diagnosis can have acute exacerbations. Ask how today's presentation compares with the client's normal baseline.

Inflammation and tissue healing

Inflammation is part of the body's response to tissue injury and infection. Common local signs include heat, redness, swelling, pain and altered function. Students should distinguish a normal healing response from a situation where massage could aggravate symptoms or where medical evaluation is indicated.

During early tissue healing, protect the area and avoid assuming that increased circulation or deeper pressure will “speed healing.” As recovery progresses, appropriately dosed movement and massage may support comfort and function when not contraindicated. Treatment intensity should follow tissue tolerance and the client's overall condition.

Pain: symptom, not diagnosis

Pain intensity alone doesn't reveal which tissue is responsible. Ask about onset, mechanism, quality, location, radiation, aggravating and relieving factors, neurological symptoms, systemic symptoms and prior evaluation. A therapist can document statements such as “client reports burning pain radiating into the hand” or “active rotation reproduces symptoms.” The therapist shouldn't convert those observations into an unauthorized medical diagnosis.

Nociceptive-type presentation
Often associated with actual or threatened tissue irritation. Location may be relatively understandable from the involved tissues.
Neuropathic-type clues
Burning, electric, shooting pain, numbness, tingling or neurological changes deserve careful screening and may require referral.
Persistent pain
Pain can be influenced by sleep, stress, sensitization, fear, activity and many biological and psychosocial factors. Avoid telling clients their pain is “all in their head.”
Referred pain
Pain may be perceived away from its source. Massage therapists should recognize patterns without claiming a medical diagnosis from a pattern alone.

Condition-based modification framework

For every pathology in this course, students should be able to answer the same questions:

  1. What is it? Know enough pathology to understand the massage implications.
  2. What might the client present with? Recognize common signs, symptoms and relevant history.
  3. What are the red flags? Know what requires postponement, referral or emergency action.
  4. Is the contraindication general or local? Don't unnecessarily deny massage when a safe modification is appropriate, and don't treat locally when the region should be avoided.
  5. What modifications make sense? Consider pressure, positioning, duration, temperature, speed, technique and treatment area.
  6. What medications matter? Think about bleeding/bruising, sensation, alertness, blood pressure, skin integrity, immune status and symptom masking.
  7. Which modality is appropriate? Select techniques based on risk, goal and training rather than automatically choosing the deepest technique.

Medical information that changes a massage

Cardiovascular
Blood-clot risk, unstable cardiovascular symptoms, edema cause, blood-pressure considerations, anticoagulants and exercise tolerance can change the plan.
Neurological
Altered sensation, weakness, neuropathy, seizures, dizziness and progressive neurological symptoms affect safety, positioning and referral decisions.
Endocrine/metabolic
Diabetes can involve altered sensation, circulation issues, skin changes and medication timing. Don't assume every person with the same diagnosis needs the same modification.
Immune/infectious
Contagious illness, fever, immunosuppression, skin infection and healing status can require postponement or specific precautions.
Musculoskeletal
Acute injury, fracture, sprain/strain, arthritis flare, surgery and tissue-healing stage guide local pressure and movement choices.
Oncology
Cancer itself isn't a blanket ban on massage. Treatment effects, blood counts, medical devices, surgery, bone involvement, lymphedema risk and individual medical guidance matter.

Technique selection belongs to clinical reasoning

Medical massage may use Swedish techniques, compression, gentle movement, stretching and other methods the therapist is trained to perform. Later blocks teach MLD and deep-tissue clinical techniques in depth. At this point students should understand that the “best” technique is the one that fits the client's presentation, goals, risk level and consent.

Important distinction: myofascial release and trigger point therapy are separate techniques. They belong with the later Deep Tissue & Clinical Techniques block rather than being treated as synonyms for medical massage.

Communication, consent and documentation

Condition-specific work still requires informed consent. Explain the plan in language the client can understand, obtain permission for changes in positioning or sensitive-area work, and invite feedback. Documentation should record relevant subjective information, objective massage-related observations, what was performed, modifications, client response and follow-up/referral recommendations without diagnosing beyond scope.

Good note
“Client reports right shoulder pain 4/10 with overhead reach. Avoided direct pressure over recent injection site. Used moderate compression to surrounding musculature. Client reports easier movement after session.”
Poor note
“Rotator cuff tear causing impingement. Fixed with deep tissue.” This claims a diagnosis and outcome that the massage therapist isn't positioned to establish.

MBLEx clinical-reasoning rule

When several answers sound possible, prioritize safety → scope → contraindications → informed consent → appropriate modification → technique. The exam often rewards the answer that gathers missing information, modifies conservatively, or refers appropriately before treatment.

Practice scenarios

1. Unilateral calf swelling

A client arrives with new calf pain, warmth and one-sided swelling after a long trip. Don't massage the leg to “improve circulation.” A possible vascular emergency must be medically evaluated.

2. Stable chronic arthritis

A client with medically managed arthritis wants massage for general comfort. Determine whether they're at baseline or in an acute inflammatory flare, ask about medications and current symptoms, and adapt pressure/positioning rather than treating the diagnosis as an automatic contraindication.

3. Peripheral neuropathy

A client reports reduced sensation in the feet. The therapist should recognize that feedback about pressure and temperature may be unreliable. Inspect skin as appropriate, avoid excessive pressure or heat, communicate carefully and modify treatment.

4. Recent surgery

A client wants work near a recent surgical site. Determine healing status, restrictions and whether clearance/guidance is needed. Don't assume “six weeks” or any single timeline makes every procedure safe.

5. Anticoagulant medication

The medication may increase bruising or bleeding risk. Massage isn't automatically forbidden, but aggressive techniques may need modification based on the client, medication effects and medical status.

Mastery check

Before moving on, you should be able to take an unfamiliar condition and reason through: pathology basics, likely presentation, red flags, general vs local contraindications, medications, positioning, pressure, duration, modality selection, documentation and referral. That's the thinking pattern that carries into the pathology, special-populations and pharmacology lessons and ultimately Theory Exam #4.
