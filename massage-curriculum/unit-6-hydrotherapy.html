Unit 6 · Hydrotherapy, Spa & Business

Hydrotherapy

The therapeutic external use of water, heat, and cold, with emphasis on safe clinical applications massage therapists may integrate into practice.

Core language

Hydrotherapy
External use of water for therapeutic, palliative, recreational or hygienic purposes.
Thermotherapy
Therapeutic use of heat.
Cryotherapy
Therapeutic use of cold.
Contrast
Alternating or combining heat and cold.

Why water works well

Water transfers thermal energy efficiently. Heat moves from warmer to cooler material through mechanisms including conduction and convection. Immersion also creates hydrostatic pressure, while water can serve as a medium for dissolved substances. Don't overstate additives such as Epsom salts when the benefit may largely come from the warm soak itself.

Cold applications

Cold can reduce pain and temporarily alter local blood flow. Common methods include cold packs and ice massage. Always use an appropriate barrier for packs, keep ice moving during ice massage, monitor sensation and skin response, and stop if the tissue response becomes concerning.

Avoid or use major caution with cold hypersensitivity, Raynaud disease, impaired circulation or sensation, previous cold injury, open wounds, and clients unable to communicate reliable feedback.

Heat applications

Heat may reduce pain and stiffness and temporarily increase tissue extensibility and range of motion. Common massage-setting applications include moist hot packs and paraffin where trained and permitted.

Burn prevention matters. Screen sensation and circulation, use sufficient insulation, monitor continuously, and don't apply heat over acute inflammation, suspected DVT, compromised tissue, topical heating agents, or areas where the client can't reliably sense temperature.

Document it

Record the area treated, modality, temperature when relevant, duration, client position, reason for use, response and outcome. Hydrotherapy is still part of the treatment plan, not an invisible add-on.

Before you move on

Quick knowledge check

Why should a therapist screen sensation before applying heat?

A client with impaired sensation may not accurately perceive excessive temperature, increasing the risk of tissue injury or burns.

What makes contrast hydrotherapy different from thermotherapy?

Contrast applications alternate or combine heat and cold rather than using heat alone.

A client has Raynaud disease. What should that make you think about?

Cold application requires major caution or avoidance because Raynaud disease involves an abnormal vascular response to cold.

What should be documented after using a hot pack?

Include the area, modality, relevant temperature, duration, positioning, reason for use, client response and outcome.
