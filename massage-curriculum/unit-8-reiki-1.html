Usui Reiki · Part One

An introduction to Reiki as a Japanese-derived complementary energy practice, including its traditional history, principles, energetic framework, practitioner preparation and basic session structure.

Mikao Usui & the tradition

Mikao Usui is identified within Reiki traditions as the founder of the Usui system in the early twentieth century. Reiki developed in Japan and later spread internationally through different teachers and lineages. Stories about Usui's spiritual experience and the origins of Reiki are part of Reiki tradition and should be presented as tradition rather than established scientific fact.

The five Reiki principles

The principles are commonly taught in forms that encourage practitioners, just for today, to release anger and worry, practice gratitude, work with integrity, and show kindness toward living things. Wording varies by translation and lineage. The point is an everyday ethical and contemplative practice, not perfect emotional control.

Traditional energetic concepts

Ki
A Japanese concept commonly translated as life-force or vital energy.
Tanden / hara
Japanese concepts associated with the body's center and used in centering and breathing practices.
Chakras
Energy-center concepts from South Asian traditions that became common in many modern Western Reiki systems. They aren't originally a uniquely Japanese Reiki concept.
Breath & attention
Used to settle the practitioner, reduce distraction and support a calm, intentional session.

Seven-chakra framework

When your Reiki lineage incorporates the modern seven-chakra model, students may learn traditional associations such as root with grounding, sacral with emotion and creativity, solar plexus with personal power, heart with connection, throat with communication, brow with intuition and crown with spiritual connection. Treat these as spiritual/energetic associations, not anatomical diagnoses.

Preparing to practice

  1. Make the environment quiet, clean and comfortable.
  2. Explain Reiki in language the receiver understands without requiring them to adopt a belief system.
  3. Ask whether the receiver prefers light touch, hands hovering above the body, or a combination.
  4. Center yourself using relaxed breathing and comfortable posture.
  5. Begin with calm, unhurried contact and avoid trying to manufacture a particular sensation.

Basic session structure

The receiver remains clothed and may lie on a massage table or sit comfortably. The practitioner works through a sequence of hand positions at or above different body regions. Sessions may be silent or include minimal communication. Consent still applies to every area of touch, and professional draping/boundary expectations don't disappear because the service is energetic rather than massage.

First practice: presence before technique

  1. Work with a partner for five minutes.
  2. Ask their preference for touch or hands-off practice.
  3. Center with several relaxed breaths.
  4. Choose three comfortable hand positions and remain at each without fidgeting or forcing an interpretation.
  5. Notice your own posture, breathing and attention.
  6. At the end, ask only neutral questions such as “How was that for you?” rather than telling the receiver what they were supposed to feel.

Reiki in professional practice

Reiki can be offered as complementary wellness care when it fits training, law and workplace policy. Don't diagnose disease through chakras or energy, guarantee healing, tell clients to stop medication, or claim that Reiki replaces appropriate healthcare. A client's spiritual worldview may be different from yours and should be respected.

Quick check

Are chakras originally a Japanese Reiki concept?

No. Chakra systems come from South Asian traditions. They have been incorporated into many modern Reiki teachings, particularly outside Japan.

A client says they don't believe in energy healing. Can they still receive Reiki?

They don't need to share the practitioner's beliefs. Explain the service neutrally, respect their preferences and let them decide whether they want it.

Can a practitioner tell a client that a blocked chakra caused their medical condition?

No. That turns a spiritual framework into an unsupported medical diagnosis and exceeds appropriate professional claims.
