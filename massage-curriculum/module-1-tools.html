Tools of the Trade

Learn how massage tables, linens, lubricants, accessories, room setup, and professional presentation affect client comfort, hygiene, and therapist longevity.

Massage tables

Portable tables fold for transport and are widely used. Stationary tables are heavier and designed to remain in a clinic or spa. A quality portable table can be just as sturdy as a stationary table.

Width
Commonly 28–33 inches. Narrower tables improve therapist reach, while wider tables can improve comfort for larger clients.
Height
Often adjustable from roughly 22–34 inches. Height should support safe therapist mechanics.
Frame
Wood frames tend to be heavier. Aluminum frames are lighter and often use faster push-button adjustments.
Padding
Density affects durability. Loft affects firmness and comfort. Higher-end tables may use multiple foam layers.
Upholstery
Polyurethane and vinyl are common. Protect upholstery from sharp objects, extreme temperatures, and incompatible cleaners.
Manufacturer
Choose a reputable company with reliable replacement parts, service, and warranty support.
Therapist thinking: the “best” table isn't simply the softest or widest. It has to balance client comfort with your ability to work without repeatedly overreaching.

Table care

  • Use linens or pads as a barrier.
  • Follow manufacturer cleaning instructions and use compatible products.
  • Address contamination appropriately and maintain hygienic surfaces.
  • Avoid prolonged storage in extreme heat or cold.
  • Inspect hinges, knobs, locks, cables, and other structural components regularly.

Accessories

Face rest
Supports the head and helps maintain comfortable cervical positioning in prone.
Arm shelf or sling
Supports the arms while the client is prone.
Bolsters & cushions
Support knees, ankles, shoulders, or other areas to reduce strain and improve positioning.
Stool
Allows seated work for areas such as the face, neck, hands, or feet.
Table warmer
Improves warmth and comfort. Secure cords so they don't create a trip hazard.
Transport equipment
Carrying cases and carts make portable tables easier to move and protect during transport.

Massage linens

Sheets, towels, blankets, face-rest covers, and bolster covers are part of both client comfort and infection-control practice.

  • Use opaque, comfortable sheets appropriate for secure draping.
  • Launder linens after every client.
  • Replace linens that remain stained, torn, damaged, or odorous.
  • Use clean face-rest and bolster coverings for each client.
  • Blankets can provide warmth without compromising draping.

Massage lubricants

Lubricant changes the amount of friction between the therapist's hands and the client's skin. Different techniques require different amounts of glide and grip.

Lotions & crèmes
Provide moisture and a balance between glide and control.
Oils
Provide longer glide but can create more laundry challenges.
Butters & balms
Thicker products used in smaller amounts and often warmed by body heat.
Gels
Can provide lighter glide and different absorption characteristics.

Hygienic dispensing

  • Pumps and squeeze tubes reduce repeated contact with bulk product.
  • Use a clean spatula to portion thick products into a small container when needed.
  • Don't return dispensed product to the original container.
  • Warm product in your hands before contacting the client.

The treatment room

Lighting
Use comfortable lighting for treatment while maintaining enough visibility for assessment and safe transfers.
Sound
Soft music or neutral background sound can reduce environmental distraction.
Temperature
Remember that a still, partially undressed client may feel colder than the moving therapist.
Safety
Keep walkways clear, secure cords, maintain non-slip surfaces, and provide adequate transfer space.

Professional appearance

Clean clothing, trimmed nails, controlled hair, appropriate footwear, and avoiding strong scents help communicate hygiene, competence, and respect for the client.

Check your understanding

1. What's the common width range for a massage table?
About 28–33 inches.
2. What's a disadvantage of a table that's too narrow for the client?
A larger client may feel less secure or comfortable, and their arms may not be adequately supported.
3. Which accessory commonly supports the knees while a client is supine?
A knee bolster.
4. Why are pumps or squeeze containers useful for lubricants?
They help limit cross-contamination and reduce unnecessary contact with the bulk product.
5. Why should treatment-room cords be secured?
To reduce trip and fall hazards for clients and therapists.
6. Name three factors to consider when choosing a massage table.
Examples include width, adjustable height, padding, frame, length, upholstery, manufacturer support, and portability.
