Curriculum / Unit 7 / Shiatsu

Shiatsu Practicum

Practice comfortable sustained pressure, whole-body rhythm and efficient body mechanics while understanding shiatsu as a Japanese bodywork tradition with multiple schools and lineages.

Set-up

Shiatsu is commonly performed through clothing on a mat or another supportive surface, though professional styles vary. Keep the receiver warm, support joints comfortably, explain that pressure and movement will be used, and make sure the receiver can communicate easily throughout the session.

Pressure principles

Sink, don't poke
Make contact first, then gradually transfer body weight.
Stay relaxed
Use alignment and body weight rather than exhausting the thumbs and shoulders.
Use rhythm
Pressure should feel intentional and connected rather than random points.
Listen through contact
Guarding, breath changes and withdrawal are feedback, even before the client speaks.

Contact surfaces

Palms create broad comfortable pressure and are useful for learning body-weight transfer. Thumbs allow more focused contact but shouldn't become the therapist's only tool. Depending on training and body region, other contact surfaces may be used. Choose the broadest comfortable tool that accomplishes the goal.

Traditional framework

Students may learn meridian or ki concepts as part of shiatsu's traditional framework. Different schools may emphasize these concepts differently. When communicating clinically, distinguish traditional theory from measurable anatomy and don't promise that pressing a point treats a specific internal-organ disease.

Safety & modification

Modify pressure over fragile tissue, acute injury, inflammation, varicosities, bony prominences or areas with altered sensation. Stop or change approach for sharp pain, numbness, tingling, vascular symptoms, guarding or any response that suggests the pressure isn't appropriate.

Partner practicum: build rhythm

  1. Position your partner comfortably and establish broad palm contact.
  2. Practice leaning in and releasing using body weight rather than muscular pushing.
  3. Create a consistent pressure rhythm across a broad region.
  4. Repeat using carefully controlled thumb pressure on an appropriate region.
  5. Add one gentle position change or stretch without breaking the sense of flow.
  6. Ask your partner to compare pressure that felt like “leaning” versus pressure that felt like “poking.”
  7. Switch roles and repeat.

Instructor check: body mechanics, relaxed contact, gradual pressure, rhythm, communication, positioning and appropriate modification.
