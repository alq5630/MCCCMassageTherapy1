Client Positioning

Position the client for comfort, stability, access, and safe therapist mechanics.

Five key positions

Prone
Client lies face down. Support the ankles as appropriate and use a properly adjusted face rest. Additional chest or breast support may improve comfort when needed.
Supine
Client lies face up. Support the head and neck as appropriate and use a bolster under the knees to reduce strain. More support may be useful for low-back discomfort.
Semi-reclining / Fowler's
The torso is elevated into a half-sitting position. This can be useful when lying flat isn't comfortable, including some prenatal or breathing-related situations.
Side-lying
Also called lateral recumbent. Support the head and use pillows or bolsters between or around the legs, chest, and arms to create a stable position.
Seated
The client may use the feet, pelvis, arms, or massage-chair supports to create a stable base. Common for chair massage and focused upper-body work.

Bolstering is problem solving

A bolster isn't automatically placed in exactly the same spot for every client. Use support to improve alignment, reduce muscular effort, protect sensitive areas, and make the position sustainable.

Safety rule: remove bolsters and other obstacles before the client gets on or off the table. Something comfortable during treatment can become a transfer hazard afterward.

Positioning lab

Set up a partner comfortably in prone and explain each support choice.
Reposition the partner into supine and adjust knee/head support based on feedback.
Create a stable side-lying setup using pillows and bolsters.
Practice a semi-reclined setup and identify situations where it might be useful.
Before every simulated transfer, scan the table and floor for bolsters, cords, stools, and other obstacles.

Check your understanding

A client lying flat on their back is in what position?

Supine.

What's another term for side-lying?

Lateral recumbent.

Why should bolsters be removed before a client gets off the table?

They can become obstacles and increase the risk of an unsafe transfer or fall.

What's the primary goal of positioning?

To create comfort and stability while allowing appropriate access and safe therapist mechanics.
