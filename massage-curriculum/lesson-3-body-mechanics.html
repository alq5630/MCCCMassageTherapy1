Body Mechanics

Good body mechanics make massage safer, more efficient and more sustainable. The goal is to organize your whole body so force comes from gravity, body weight and coordinated movement rather than repeatedly muscling pressure through your hands.

What body mechanics means

Body mechanics is the use of your body in ways that are safe, energy-conserving, anatomically and physiologically efficient, and less physically stressful. Effective mechanics provide support, balance and mobility while you work.

Ergonomics means adapting the work to the worker. Don't force your body to fit the table, technique or room. Adjust the work environment when you can.

Prepare physically and mentally

Physical wellness
Regular activity, balanced nutrition and adequate sleep support the physical demands of massage work.
Warm up
A brief 5–10 minute warmup can use massage-specific movements such as squats, lunges, shoulder/wrist/ankle circles, spinal flexion-extension and reach-and-pull patterns.
Manage workload
Allow sufficient time between massages when possible and alternate more physically intensive treatments with less intensive work.
Mindfulness
Pay attention to tension, fatigue and discomfort as they appear. Early awareness lets you change mechanics before strain becomes a pattern.

Mental fatigue matters too. Focused attention to the present moment can help you notice when you've started gripping, shrugging, twisting, holding your breath or working from an awkward position.

Set up the environment for your body

Workspace, table height, flooring, clothing and footwear all affect mechanics. The source material recommends roughly 3 feet / 1 meter of working space around the table when the room allows it.

Table height
Adjust for the client's size, the body region being treated and the technique. Compressive work may require a lower table than lighter work.
Attire
Wear professional clothing that allows free movement. Supportive shoes should allow you to stand and shift weight comfortably.

Foot stances and base of support

Your base of support includes the points of contact supporting you. Choose a stance based on target location, technique, excursion and the direction your body needs to travel.

Archer stance
Feet are asymmetric and point in different directions. The lead leg is positioned through hip external rotation. Use it when the target is toward your side or when you need to lunge forward and backward through a longer technique.
Horse stance
Feet are more symmetric and point in the same general direction. Use it when the target is directly in front of you. Foot spacing can change with the task.
In either stance: keep the spine organized and shoulders comfortably dropped. The stance is a moving base, not a frozen pose. Shift weight instead of reaching from the shoulder or bending repeatedly from the low back.

Core stability and center of gravity

Core stability is the ability to control trunk position and motion so force can be produced, transferred and controlled efficiently. The functional core includes the spine, pelvic girdle and musculature of the trunk, hips and pelvic floor.

An aligned spine, flexed hips and controlled pelvic position help stabilize the trunk. Think of the core as the engine that transfers movement outward to the arms and hands.

Your center of gravity is the point around which body mass is balanced. Bringing the center of gravity closer to the ground generally increases balance and stability. For massage, this is one reason soft knees, lunging and lowering your body are usually better than bending at the waist.

Leverage gravity and body weight

Force is a push or pull resulting from interaction between objects. Massage commonly uses compressive forces and, when appropriate and within scope, decompressive or traction forces.

Compression
Position yourself near and over the application area. Stack joints when possible and direct force toward the tissue, often near a perpendicular or 90° relationship to the surface. Let body weight contribute instead of pressing only with arm muscles.
Decompression / traction
Direct force away from the client's body. Use a stable stance, an erect organized spine and controlled core engagement while shifting your body backward rather than yanking with the arms.
Pressure should travel through your structure. If your shoulders are doing all the work, change your feet, table height, distance from the client or contact surface.

When the client isn't on a table

Body mechanics still matter when working with a client seated or on the floor. Keep your abdomen oriented toward the direction of movement when possible and adapt with kneeling, sitting on the heels, crawling, squatting or lunging positions rather than twisting to reach the target.

Practice in lab

Adjust table height for light gliding work, then for stronger compression. Explain why you changed it.
Perform a long stroke in archer stance and create the excursion by shifting your body rather than reaching.
Use horse stance for a target directly in front of you and keep your shoulders relaxed.
Compare arm-generated compression with compression created through stacked joints and body-weight transfer.
Practice a gentle traction setup by shifting backward through an archer stance without rounding or twisting your spine.
Have a partner call out gripping, locked knees, elevated shoulders, wrist deviation, overreaching or breath holding when they see it.
Experiment with lowering your center of gravity while keeping the spine organized.

MBLEx-style check

What does ergonomics mean?
Adapting the work to the worker.
Which stance is generally useful when the target is directly in front of the therapist?
Horse stance.
Which stance is useful for a long technique requiring forward and backward weight shift?
Archer stance.
What is core stability?
The ability to control trunk position and motion for efficient production, transfer and control of force and movement.
How should a therapist generate stronger compression efficiently?
Move near and over the target, organize and stack the joints, and transfer body weight through the contact rather than relying primarily on arm strength.
Why can lowering the center of gravity improve mechanics?
A lower center of gravity generally increases balance and stability.
