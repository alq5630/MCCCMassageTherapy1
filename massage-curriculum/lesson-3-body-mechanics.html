Body Mechanics

Use your body safely and efficiently so pressure comes from coordinated movement and body weight rather than muscular effort alone.

In this lessonPrinciplesStancesCore & gravityUsing body weightLabMBLEx check

What body mechanics means

Body mechanics is using your body in ways that are safe, energy-conserving, anatomically efficient, and less physically stressful. For massage therapists, good mechanics help reduce unnecessary strain while allowing effective contact with the client.

Prepare physically
Warm up, maintain mobility and strength, and support yourself with sleep, hydration, nutrition, and exercise.
Prepare mentally
Notice tension and fatigue while you work. Adjust your position before strain becomes your default.
Prepare the room
Leave enough space to move around the table and adjust table height for the client and technique.
Dress to move
Professional clothing should permit lunging, squatting, reaching, and weight shifting. Supportive footwear matters too.

Your base of support

Archer stance
A lunge-like stance with one foot forward and one back. Useful for long strokes and work that travels along the table.
Horse stance
Feet wider than hip distance and relatively parallel, with knees flexed. Useful when the target is directly in front of you.
Think movement, not posing. A stance gives you a stable base, but you should still shift your weight rather than freezing your legs and reaching with your upper body.

Core stability & center of gravity

Core stability is your ability to control the position and motion of your trunk. A stable trunk helps transfer force through the body instead of asking the shoulders, wrists, and low back to create all the pressure.

Your center of gravity is the point around which body weight is balanced. In general, lowering your center of gravity and widening your base increases stability.

Let gravity help

Compression / pushing
Shift body weight toward the tissue rather than pressing primarily with the arms.
Traction / pulling
Use a stable stance and shift backward while maintaining an organized spine.

When working seated, on the floor, or in unusual positions, adapt with squatting, lunging, kneeling, or crawling patterns and keep your torso oriented toward the direction of movement when possible.

Practice in lab

Find a workable table height for broad back strokes, then explain why it works for your body.
Perform a long effleurage-style movement using an archer stance and deliberate weight shift.
Work on an area directly in front of you using horse stance without locking your knees.
Compare arm-generated pressure with pressure generated by shifting body weight.
Have your partner identify when you're reaching, twisting, locking joints, or losing your base of support.
