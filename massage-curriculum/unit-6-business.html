Curriculum / Unit 6 / Business of Massage

Business of Massage

Being excellent at massage and running a sustainable massage practice are two different skill sets. A professional needs both.

1. Choose how you'll work

Employee
Usually works under greater employer direction and receives a W-2 with payroll withholding and whatever benefits the employer provides.
Independent contractor
Operates an independent business relationship and generally manages their own taxes and business expenses. Calling someone a contractor doesn't automatically make the classification correct.
Sole proprietorship
The simplest one-owner business form. The owner and business aren't separate legal entities for liability purposes.
LLC
A state-law entity that can create legal separation between business and personal assets when properly maintained. Tax treatment is a separate question.
Partnership
Two or more owners share the business. Ownership, money, authority, responsibilities and exit terms should be clearly documented.
Corporation / tax elections
S-corp and C-corp terminology involves tax and corporate structure choices that require more planning than simply registering a business name.

Key distinction: legal structure, tax treatment and worker classification aren't the same thing.

2. Before you open the door

Research state and local licensing requirements, business registration, fictitious-name rules when applicable, zoning, occupancy, local permits, sales-tax obligations for taxable products or services, professional requirements, and facility rules. Requirements depend on where and how you practice.

Separate the money
Use dedicated business banking and a consistent bookkeeping system.
Build your records
Track revenue, expenses, receipts, mileage, equipment, continuing education and other legitimate business records.
Protect client information
Choose secure systems and collect only information you actually need.
Know your contracts
Read leases, room-rental agreements, employment agreements and contractor agreements before signing.

3. Insurance is part of risk management

CoverageWhat it generally addresses
Professional liabilityClaims involving professional services, alleged negligence or injury related to treatment.
General liabilityMany premises and non-treatment risks, such as a client slipping in the office.
Product liabilityClaims related to products sold, supplied or used.
PropertyBusiness equipment and property losses covered by the policy.
Business interruptionCertain covered losses that interrupt normal operations.
CyberSome risks involving electronic data, breaches or cyber incidents.
DisabilityIncome protection can be especially important in a physically demanding profession.

Coverage varies. A therapist should understand exclusions, limits and whether a policy follows them across all work locations.

4. Price from the math, not from fear

Monthly break-even revenue = monthly fixed costs + expected variable costs. To turn that into a practical session target, divide the amount the business must earn by the average amount retained per appointment.

Fixed expenses
Rent, software, insurance, phone, recurring memberships and other costs that don't change much with each session.
Variable expenses
Laundry, linens, lubricant, disposables, merchant fees and other costs affected by service volume.
Don't confuse revenue with pay
Money collected must also cover expenses and taxes before it becomes usable owner income.
Capacity matters
A revenue plan isn't realistic if it requires more hands-on sessions than your schedule or body can sustainably perform.

5. Taxes and recordkeeping

Employees typically have payroll taxes withheld. Self-employed therapists generally need to plan for their own income and self-employment tax obligations and may need estimated tax payments. Keep organized records instead of trying to reconstruct an entire year at tax time.

Track: gross income, tips when applicable, business expenses, mileage, equipment purchases, continuing education, professional fees, insurance, rent and other records relevant to the practice. Tax rules change and individual situations differ, so students should learn when to use a qualified tax professional rather than treating social media as tax advice.

6. Policies are business boundaries

Write policies before you need them. Address cancellation and no-shows, late arrivals, payment, refunds, packages and gift certificates, illness, communication methods, inappropriate behavior, draping and professional conduct. Apply policies consistently while leaving room for legitimate professional judgment.

A good policy answers three questions: What happens? Why? How is it communicated?

7. Marketing without nonsense

Define who you serve, what problem or experience they're seeking, why your practice is a good fit and how they'll find you. Your marketing should match your actual scope, training and services.

Good marketing
Clear service descriptions, professional photos, useful education, community relationships, referral systems, consistent branding, ethical testimonials and an easy booking process.
Bad marketing
Diagnosing, guaranteeing outcomes, claiming to cure disease, exaggerating credentials, fear-based claims, copying competitors or using client information without permission.

Retention isn't just marketing. It's competent care, trust, communication, reliability, appropriate follow-up and an experience clients want to repeat.

8. Know your numbers

Every practice should be able to answer: How much does it cost to operate each month? How many appointments are needed to break even? What's the average revenue per appointment? What's the rebooking rate? Which services are profitable? How much cash reserve exists? How much should be set aside for taxes?

Business project

Build a realistic massage business from the ground up. Your plan should include:

  1. Executive summary and business concept
  2. Legal structure and employment model
  3. Location, zoning and facility considerations
  4. Target market and ideal-client profile
  5. Competitor analysis and SWOT analysis
  6. Service menu, session lengths and pricing rationale
  7. Startup budget and sources of funding
  8. Monthly fixed and variable expenses
  9. Break-even calculation
  10. 12-month revenue projection with realistic appointment capacity
  11. Marketing budget and client-acquisition plan
  12. Cancellation, payment, conduct and communication policies
  13. Insurance and risk-management plan
  14. Tax and recordkeeping plan
  15. Professional organizations, mentors and referral relationships
  16. Short-term and long-term business goals

Reality check: If your plan only works when every appointment slot stays full, no one cancels, nothing breaks and you never take a day off, revise the plan.

Case challenge

A therapist rents a room for $900 per month, pays $350 in other fixed business expenses and averages $12 in variable costs per session. Their average collected service revenue is $90 per appointment. Before considering taxes or owner pay, how many sessions are needed each month to cover those stated operating costs? Then identify at least three expenses or financial realities the simplified calculation still leaves out.
