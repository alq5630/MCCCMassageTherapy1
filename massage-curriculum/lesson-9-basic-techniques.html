Basic Massage Techniques

The name of a stroke is only the beginning. Pressure, direction, excursion, rate, rhythm, duration, sequence, contact and body mechanics determine what the technique actually feels like and whether it fits the client.

Technique = movement + variables + intention

Two therapists can perform “effleurage” and create completely different experiences. Students need to know both the technique family and the variables controlling its application. Massage uses compression, traction, gliding, lifting, shearing, percussion and oscillation in different combinations. Client condition and comfort can change the technique, procedure and position at any point.

The variables you control

Pressure
Physical force from light to deep. Appropriate pressure depends on client preference, tissue, region, goal, health status and response.
Direction
The path of force. Many extremity glides are taught centripetally, while friction may move across, parallel to or around fibers.
Excursion
The distance traveled. A stroke can cover a tiny focused region, one muscle, an entire limb or a broad body region.
Rate
How quickly a technique is performed or repeated. Slower work often feels more calming; faster work can feel more stimulating.
Rhythm
The predictability and regularity of movement. Consistent rhythm helps the client anticipate contact and relax.
Duration
How long a technique or session lasts. Duration changes with goals, tolerance and available time.
Frequency
How often treatment occurs. This isn't the same as rate, which describes speed within a session.
Sequence
The order in which techniques are arranged.
Routine
A repeatable combination of techniques and sequences. Your New Routine provides structure but still has to adapt to the client.
Contact surface
Fingers, thumbs, palms, fists, forearms and other trained contacts change force distribution and therapist effort.

Pressure without the ego

Light
Small tissue displacement. Useful for introductory contact, lubricant, sensitive tissue and lighter nervous-system input.
Medium
Clear tissue engagement without forcing through resistance. Often useful for broad muscular work.
Deep
Greater tissue displacement using efficient body-weight transfer. Best applied where anatomy, tissue density, goals and client tolerance support it.

Deep pressure isn't automatically more therapeutic. Students should be able to increase pressure while keeping wrists aligned, shoulders relaxed, breath easy and client feedback available.

“No pain, no gain” isn't a massage principle

Pressure should feel purposeful and tolerable. Sharp, electrical, burning, alarming or escalating symptoms are reasons to stop or modify. A client who holds their breath, guards, pulls away or repeatedly says the pressure is intense is giving you treatment information.

Effleurage: more than “rub up the muscle”

Uses
Introduce touch, spread lubricant, assess tissue, connect techniques, warm broad regions and reassess after focused work.
Mechanics
Face the direction you're traveling, keep wrists neutral and generate deeper pressure through body-weight transfer rather than hand strain.
Continuous contact
Direction changes should feel intentional. Avoid abrupt stops or accidental loss of contact unless the sequence requires it.
Nerve stroke
A very light finishing variation. Light doesn't mean barely touching or tickling the client.

Petrissage: lift, compress, release

Petrissage comes from the idea of kneading. The defining feature is not merely moving the hands in circles. Tissue is grasped or gathered, lifted when appropriate, compressed and released while maintaining a comfortable rhythm.

Superficial
Primarily engages skin and superficial tissue. Skin rolling is one example.
Deep
Engages a muscle or muscle group more fully. Broad palmar contact is often more sustainable than relying on fingertips.
Fulling
Hands move tissue laterally while lifting/compressing, useful where a muscle mass can be safely gathered.
Common error
Pinching with fingertips instead of gathering tissue with the hand and using the body efficiently.

Friction: know which kind you're doing

Superficial warming friction
Hands move briskly over the skin to create warming sensory input.
Deep friction
The therapist engages the skin so skin and hand move together over deeper tissue rather than simply sliding across the surface.
Transverse friction
Pressure moves perpendicular to the fibers being addressed. Also called cross-fiber friction.
Chucking friction
Pressure moves parallel to the fibers being addressed.
Circular friction
Small circular tissue engagement, often useful around appropriate joints or bony contours.
Lubricant
Focused friction usually requires little or no lubricant so the therapist can move tissue rather than slide over it.

Compression, tapotement & vibration

Sustained compression
Gradually load tissue, hold the contact, monitor response and ease out. This action appears in several advanced techniques but isn't automatically trigger point therapy.
Rhythmic compression
Repeated pressing and releasing. Through-drape compression can be useful for broad opening work.
Tapotement
Keep the hands and wrists relaxed enough to create rhythm. Percussion should never become pounding on vulnerable structures.
Vibration
Transfer a controlled oscillating movement into the client. Use your body efficiently so the technique doesn't become a forearm endurance contest.

Technique identity matters in the New Routine

Your routine nicknames make the sequence easier to remember, but students still need to identify the professional technique underneath the nickname. “What are my hands doing?” should always have an anatomical and technique-based answer.

Glides / washes
Usually variations of effleurage or broad gliding contact. Students should identify pressure, direction and purpose.
Kneads / wrings
May involve petrissage, friction or a combination depending on whether tissue is lifted, compressed, glided or sheared.
Cross-fiber work
Falls within friction when the tissue is engaged and moved transverse to fiber direction.
Compressions / holds
Non-gliding pressure or still contact. A hold isn't automatically “nothing.” Intent, force and duration matter.

Body mechanics are part of the technique

Face your work
Orient the trunk toward the direction of movement instead of twisting repeatedly.
Stack joints
Keep wrist, elbow and shoulder relationships efficient when loading pressure.
Move from your legs
Shift body weight rather than repeatedly pushing from the shoulders and thumbs.
Stabilize when needed
When gliding deeply along a limb, use the opposite hand or positioning to prevent the limb from sliding away.

Technique identification lab

Perform one effleurage variation and name pressure, direction, excursion, rate and purpose.
Demonstrate petrissage that clearly lifts/compresses tissue rather than pinching.
Demonstrate superficial warming friction, transverse friction and circular friction and explain the difference.
Perform sustained compression and rhythmic compression on an appropriate region.
Demonstrate safe rhythmic tapotement with relaxed wrists.
Perform vibration or shaking without creating unnecessary tension in your own hands.
Choose three New Routine nicknames and identify the foundational technique family underneath each.
Repeat a short sequence while your partner changes pressure preference. Adapt without breaking the flow.
Instructor checks body mechanics, rhythm, contact quality, anatomical safety and client communication.

MBLEx + practical check

What's the difference between pressure and excursion?

Pressure is the amount of physical force. Excursion is the distance the technique travels.

What distinguishes petrissage from effleurage?

Petrissage involves compression with lifting/decompression and release of soft tissue. Effleurage is primarily a gliding/stroking technique.

What happens during deep friction?

The therapist engages the skin so the hand and skin move together over deeper underlying tissue, creating localized shearing rather than simply sliding over the surface.

Transverse friction moves in what relationship to the fibers?

Perpendicular or at right angles to the fibers.

Is sustained compression automatically trigger point therapy?

No. Compression is a foundational technique family. Trigger point therapy adds a specific assessment model, clinical target and reassessment process.

Why is rhythm clinically important?

Predictable rhythmic contact helps the client anticipate the movement and makes transitions feel intentional rather than hesitant or abrupt.

What makes deep pressure safer for the therapist?

Appropriate table height, joint stacking, body-weight transfer, broad contact surfaces when possible and avoiding repetitive force from small hand muscles.
