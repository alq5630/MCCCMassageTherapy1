Trigger Point Therapy

Trigger point therapy is taught as its own focused clinical technique. It isn't interchangeable with myofascial release. Students learn to recognize a clinically meaningful tender point, compare symptoms with referral patterns, apply tolerable sustained pressure, reassess, and know when the client's symptoms don't fit a muscular explanation.

What is a trigger point?

The classic Travell and Simons model describes a myofascial trigger point as a hypersensitive spot associated with a palpable nodule in a taut band of skeletal muscle. Clinically, students may encounter localized tenderness, familiar referred symptoms, restricted motion or altered muscle function. Trigger-point terminology is widely used in manual therapy, but the exact biological model and reliability of palpation remain debated. Treat the model as a useful clinical framework rather than proof that every painful “knot” has one single mechanism.

Trigger point vs. general tenderness

A sore spot isn't automatically a trigger point. Build the assessment from several findings: a taut or unusually tense band, a distinctly sensitive point, reproduction of the client's familiar symptoms, a recognizable referral pattern, and a functional finding such as restricted ROM. Client feedback matters more than forcing the tissue to fit a chart.

Types commonly taught

Active trigger point
Associated with spontaneous or movement-related symptoms and may reproduce familiar local or referred pain when palpated.
Latent trigger point
May be tender when compressed and associated with stiffness or altered function without being the client's current spontaneous pain complaint.
Satellite trigger point
A secondary sensitive point described as developing in another muscle within the functional or referral pattern of a primary problem.
Key clinical rule
These labels organize reasoning. They don't replace differential screening for nerve, joint, vascular or systemic causes of symptoms.

Why trigger points may develop

Common clinical associations include repetitive overload, sustained postures, direct trauma, protective guarding, abrupt increases in activity, insufficient recovery and persistent stress-related muscle tension. Current models have proposed altered motor-endplate activity, local metabolic changes, nociceptive sensitization and central nervous-system involvement. No single mechanism completely explains every painful palpable point.

Referred pain

Referred pain is perceived away from the tissue being stimulated. Trigger-point maps describe recurring referral patterns, but maps are guides rather than diagnostic guarantees. A point in upper trapezius may refer toward the head; gluteal points may produce symptoms into the hip or leg; forearm points may be felt toward the wrist or hand. Similar symptom distributions can also arise from nerve irritation, joint pathology or other conditions.

Assessment sequence

  1. Start with the complaint. Ask location, quality, onset, aggravating/relieving factors and whether symptoms travel.
  2. Screen first. Rule out red flags and reasons focused pressure is inappropriate.
  3. Observe movement. Compare active ROM, guarding and obvious compensation.
  4. Palpate broadly before specifically. Identify the muscle and general tone before hunting for a point.
  5. Locate a taut/tender area. Palpate across and along fibers with controlled pressure.
  6. Ask whether it reproduces familiar symptoms. “Tender” and “that's my pain” aren't the same answer.
  7. Establish a baseline. Record pain rating, ROM or another meaningful comparison.
  8. Treat and reassess. A useful intervention should be evaluated by what changes afterward.

Manual pressure release

Use a broad, controlled contact with thumb, reinforced finger, knuckle or another safe contact appropriate to the tissue. Gradually sink to a tolerable level rather than jabbing into the point. Maintain pressure while monitoring the client's response, commonly for tens of seconds up to roughly a minute or longer when appropriate. Pressure may be adjusted as sensitivity changes.

The goal isn't to create maximum pain or “crush a knot.” Aim for tolerable therapeutic intensity that doesn't cause guarding, breath holding, sharp pain, numbness, tingling or protective withdrawal.

Pressure scale and communication

A 0–10 scale can help students communicate, but don't chase a particular number. Ask about quality as well as intensity. Dull, achy, familiar referral may be acceptable when the client agrees. Sharp, electric, burning, numb, tingling, nauseating or threatening sensations are signals to reduce or stop pressure and reassess.

After the pressure release

Re-palpate gently and repeat the original movement or ROM test. Stretching, active movement, heat or another technique may be integrated when appropriate. Don't declare success simply because the point feels softer under the therapist's thumb. The meaningful question is whether symptoms, movement or function changed.

Contraindications and precautions

Avoid focused trigger-point pressure over acute injury/inflammation, infection, open wounds, unstable fractures, significant bruising, suspected thrombosis, compromised vascular tissue or areas where sensation is significantly impaired. Modify for medications and conditions that increase bruising/bleeding risk, fragile tissue, chronic pain sensitization, pregnancy positioning needs and client tolerance. Never aggressively compress endangerment sites or major neurovascular structures.

Trigger point therapy isn't diagnosis

Reproducing a symptom doesn't prove the muscle is the sole cause. Tingling into the hand may involve muscle referral, but it can also involve cervical radiculopathy or peripheral nerve compression. Leg symptoms may involve gluteal referral, but they may also be neurologic or vascular. Massage therapists identify massage-relevant findings, recognize patterns, modify treatment and refer when the presentation exceeds scope.

Trigger point therapy vs. MFR

Trigger point therapy
Usually targets a discrete hypersensitive point/taut band, often uses sustained compression, tracks local or referred symptoms and reassesses the point and function.
Myofascial release
Engages a broader fascial restriction with sustained stretch, traction or shear and follows tissue resistance over time. The target and hand mechanics are different.

The techniques can be used in the same treatment plan, but one shouldn't be taught as merely another name for the other.

Clinical example

A client reports headaches after long computer days. Rather than immediately pressing upper trapezius trigger points, the therapist screens the headache, checks neck/shoulder movement, palpates relevant tissues, and asks whether pressure reproduces the familiar symptom. If a tolerable upper-trapezius point reproduces the familiar referral, pressure release may be followed by movement and reassessment. A sudden severe headache, neurologic signs or an unfamiliar headache pattern changes the plan from treatment to referral reasoning.

Common student mistakes

Hunting for pain
More tenderness doesn't mean better assessment.
Using maximum pressure
Excessive pressure increases guarding and can irritate tissue.
Ignoring referral quality
Ask whether the sensation is familiar and where it travels.
Skipping reassessment
Always compare with the baseline finding.
Treating the chart
Referral maps support reasoning. The client in front of you matters more.
Calling every knot a trigger point
General muscle tension, tender tissue and trigger-point findings aren't identical.

Check yourself

What's the most useful client response during palpation?

Recognition that the pressure reproduces the client's familiar symptom pattern, considered alongside other assessment findings.

Should trigger point work hurt?

It can be tender or intense, but therapeutic pressure shouldn't require overwhelming pain. Sharp, electric, numb or threatening sensations are reasons to modify or stop.

Does referred pain prove a trigger point is causing the client's condition?

No. Referral patterns support a working hypothesis but don't exclude neurologic, joint, vascular or systemic causes.

Are trigger point therapy and MFR the same?

No. They can complement one another but use different targets, contacts and clinical reasoning.
