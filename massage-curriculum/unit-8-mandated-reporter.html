Recognizing & Reporting Child Abuse

Pennsylvania health-related licensees are mandated reporters. This lesson reinforces professional readiness, but it does not replace the DHS-approved training required for licensure.

What Pennsylvania requires

Applicants for an initial health-related professional license must complete 3 hours of DHS-approved child-abuse recognition and reporting training. Biennial renewal requires 2 hours of approved training. The approved provider reports completion electronically, so students should use a currently approved course and keep their own completion records.

The threshold is reasonable cause to suspect

Your role is not to investigate or prove abuse. A mandated reporter makes a report when the legal reporting threshold is met. Don't delay a required report while trying to gather certainty, interrogate a child, confront a suspected perpetrator or conduct your own investigation.

Situations that can trigger the duty to report

Professional contact
You encounter a child in the course of your work or professional practice and have reasonable cause to suspect abuse.
Responsibility for the child
You are responsible for care, supervision, guidance or training, or are affiliated with an entity that is.
Specific disclosure
Someone specifically tells you that an identifiable child is a victim of abuse.
Disclosure by a person 14+
An individual age 14 or older specifically discloses that they committed child abuse.

A report can be required even when the child didn't personally disclose the abuse and even when you haven't directly met the child.

How mandated reporters report in Pennsylvania

Use Pennsylvania's current Child Welfare Portal or call ChildLine at 1-800-932-0313. Mandated reporters identify themselves and provide contact information. If a child appears to be in immediate danger or there is an emergency, activate emergency services as appropriate in addition to fulfilling reporting responsibilities.

When a child discloses

  1. Stay calm and listen.
  2. Don't react with disbelief, blame or shock.
  3. Don't promise secrecy.
  4. Use minimal, non-leading clarification rather than an interview.
  5. Don't make the child repeatedly retell the story.
  6. Record relevant information according to workplace policy without adding assumptions.
  7. Make the required report promptly through the current Pennsylvania process.

Massage-specific boundaries with minors

Massage involves touch, privacy and partial undressing, so consent, draping and communication need to be especially clear. Follow applicable laws and facility policies for parent or guardian involvement while preserving the minor client's dignity and right to communicate discomfort. A parent consenting to the appointment doesn't mean the minor loses the ability to say stop.

Scenario lab

For each scenario, students decide: What do I know? What am I assuming? Is there reasonable cause to suspect abuse? What should I do next?

  1. A teenage client quietly tells you that an adult at home hurt their younger sibling.
  2. You notice bruising on a child during massage and the explanation changes several times.
  3. An adult client tells you they abused an identifiable child.
  4. A minor discloses something concerning and asks you to promise not to tell anyone.

The exercise is about recognizing the reporting decision, not diagnosing injuries or deciding whether an allegation is true.

Course completion

Complete the program-assigned Pennsylvania-approved training. State law, approved providers and procedures can change, so students should verify current requirements through Pennsylvania DHS and the State Board rather than relying on an old screenshot or handout.
