Intake, Treatment Planning & SOAP Notes

Clinical reasoning starts before the client gets on the table. Assessment, consent, scope, communication, treatment planning, reassessment, referral and documentation are part of the massage.

Before massage begins

Decisions are shaped by the client's goals and preferences, health history, current symptoms, medications, recent injuries or procedures, prior response to massage, relevant evidence, and the therapist's training. Safety, informed consent and scope come first.

Ask
What brings the client in? What changed? What makes symptoms better or worse? What are they hoping massage will accomplish?
Screen
Look for information that changes whether, where or how massage should be performed.
Plan
Choose realistic goals, regions, techniques, pressure and positioning based on the current presentation.
Reassess
Compare the client's response with the starting point and use it to guide the next decision.

Scope of practice

Massage-therapy role
Gather relevant information, observe and palpate within training, identify massage-related findings, plan a session, document, educate within scope, and refer when appropriate.
Don't cross the line
Don't diagnose disease, prescribe medication, perform procedures requiring another license, or present massage findings as medical diagnoses.

Assessment in massage therapy answers questions such as “Is massage appropriate?” and “How should I modify this session?” It doesn't turn the massage therapist into a medical diagnostician.

The client interview

Open-ended
“Tell me what you've been noticing in your shoulder.”
Closed-ended
“Is the pain present right now?”
Exploratory
“What movements seem to make it worse?”
Reflective
“So it feels better with movement but worse after sitting?”

Use neutral language. Leading questions can shape a client's answer and reduce the quality of the information you gather.

Pain is data, not a challenge

Use an appropriate pain scale when pain is relevant. A high, sudden, unexplained or unusual pain report should change your reasoning. Don't assume every painful area needs deeper pressure. Sharp, electric, burning, numb, tingling, severe or rapidly changing symptoms may require modification or referral depending on the presentation.

Red-flag thinking

The MBLEx may describe symptoms without naming the condition. Pay attention to combinations such as unexplained swelling and heat, sudden severe symptoms, shortness of breath, chest pain, neurological changes, fever or systemic illness, recent significant trauma, or other findings that make routine massage inappropriate. You don't need to diagnose the disease to recognize that the safest next step may be to postpone and refer.

Treatment planning

A useful treatment plan identifies the client's goal, session goal, areas included or avoided, positioning, techniques, pressure, duration, relevant precautions, reassessment method and referral needs. Goals should be observable enough to reassess. “Decrease reported neck discomfort from 6/10 to a more comfortable level and reassess cervical rotation” is more useful than “fix the neck.”

PICO helps form an evidence-search question: Patient/Population/Problem, Intervention, Comparison/Control, Outcome.

SOAP documentation

S · Subjective
What the client reports: symptoms, goals, changes, preferences and relevant history.
O · Objective
What you observe, measure or palpate within scope.
A · Assessment
Your massage-therapy interpretation of relevant findings and response, without diagnosing disease.
P · Plan
Future session approach, reassessment, appropriate home care or referral.

Write factual, concise notes. Avoid judgmental language, unsupported conclusions and unnecessary personal details. Correct records according to professional and workplace policy rather than silently altering the original entry.

MBLEx scenarios

A client says, “My neck feels tight after working at my desk.” Which SOAP category is that?

Subjective. It's information reported by the client.

You observe visibly limited cervical rotation. Which category?

Objective. It's an observable finding.

A client reports sudden chest pressure and shortness of breath during intake. What's the priority?

Massage isn't the priority. Stop the intake and activate appropriate emergency response rather than trying to determine a musculoskeletal cause.

A client asks you to diagnose whether their tingling hand is carpal tunnel syndrome. What's the best response?

Explain that diagnosing the condition is outside massage scope. You can document the reported symptoms, modify appropriately, and recommend evaluation by an appropriate healthcare professional when indicated.

After treatment, the client's pain rating changes from 6/10 to 3/10 and rotation improves. Why document both?

They provide subjective and objective reassessment information that helps evaluate the session and plan future care.

What does standard of care mean?

The level and type of care expected from a reasonably competent professional with similar training working under similar circumstances.
