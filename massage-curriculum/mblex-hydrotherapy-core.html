Heat, Cold & Water Applications

Know what thermotherapy and cryotherapy do, when they may help, when they may be unsafe, how water transfers heat and what must be documented.

Core definitions

Hydrotherapy
External use of water for therapeutic, palliative, recreational or hygienic purposes.
Thermotherapy
Therapeutic use of heat.
Cryotherapy
Therapeutic use of cold.
Contrast method
Alternates or combines heat and cold applications according to the intended protocol.

Water and heat transfer

Conduction
Heat transfer through direct contact, such as a warm pack contacting a covered body region.
Convection
Heat transfer through circulating fluid or air.
Evaporation
Liquid changes to vapor and removes heat from a surface.
Hydrostatic pressure
Pressure exerted by fluid on an immersed object. Pressure increases with depth.

Cold applications

Cold can reduce superficial tissue temperature and alter nerve conduction, pain perception and local blood flow. Effects depend on method, duration, tissue depth and individual response.

Methods
Cold packs, ice packs, ice massage and selected contrast applications.
Expected sensations
Clients may experience intense cold followed by aching/stinging and numbness. Monitor continuously rather than treating numbness as permission to leave cold unattended.

Heat applications

Superficial heat can increase local skin/tissue temperature and blood flow, support comfort, reduce perceived stiffness and temporarily improve stretch tolerance or ROM in some contexts.

Methods
Warm/hot packs, warm water applications and paraffin are common examples.
Stretch timing
If heat is being used to support comfortable flexibility, stretching/mobilization is commonly performed while tissues remain warm rather than long after the application ends.

Thermal safety matters more than the protocol

Do not apply heat or cold where the client cannot reliably sense temperature, where circulation is significantly compromised, over suspicious vascular conditions such as DVT, or over tissue that cannot safely tolerate the modality. Open wounds, active skin problems, acute injury, previous thermal injury, implanted devices and relevant medical conditions can change the decision. Use the specific modality's contraindications rather than assuming “heat relaxes” or “ice fixes inflammation.”

Paraffin

Paraffin baths use heated wax mixtures to deliver superficial warmth, commonly to hands or feet. Remove jewelry, screen skin and sensation, follow equipment temperature/protocol and prevent contamination. Paraffin is not appropriate over open or infected skin.

Documentation

Document the modality, body region, reason for use, relevant temperature when applicable, duration, client position, precautions and the client's response/outcome. Hydrotherapy is still part of the treatment plan, not a decorative spa extra.

MBLEx rapid reasoning

What is thermotherapy?

Therapeutic application of heat.

What is cryotherapy?

Therapeutic application of cold.

Which heat-transfer method involves direct contact?

Conduction.

Why is impaired sensation a major thermal precaution?

The client may not reliably detect excessive heat or cold before tissue injury occurs.

A client has a hot, swollen unilateral calf and asks for a heating pad before massage. Appropriate?

No. A suspicious vascular presentation such as possible DVT requires medical evaluation, not heat or massage to the area.

What should be documented after hydrotherapy?

The treatment/modality, area, relevant parameters such as temperature and duration, positioning, reason, precautions and client response.
