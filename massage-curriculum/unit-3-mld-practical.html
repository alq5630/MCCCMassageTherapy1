Manual Lymphatic Drainage Practical

Translate lymphatic anatomy into a deliberate introductory sequence. Every stroke should answer three questions: what territory am I working, where does it drain, and have I prepared the proximal pathway first?

Practical, not Theory Exam content

This lesson leads to the MLD Practical Competency. Students are evaluated on screening, pressure, rhythm, direction, sequence, anatomy reasoning, communication and scope rather than a numbered written exam.

Technique standard

Use slow, rhythmic, feather-light skin stretch with relaxed hands. The pressure should move skin without sinking into muscle. Avoid heavy lubricant because too much glide makes controlled skin stretch difficult. Maintain comfortable draping and obtain consent before chest, abdominal or groin-adjacent work.

Before touching the client

  1. Review health history and ask about current swelling, infection, fever, vascular history, recent surgery, cancer treatment, heart/kidney problems and unexplained symptoms.
  2. Observe for asymmetry, redness, unusual heat, skin changes, wounds and obvious swelling without diagnosing the cause.
  3. Explain that classroom MLD is gentle superficial work and isn't treatment for diagnosed lymphedema.
  4. Position and bolster the client so the body is relaxed and breathing is unrestricted.
  5. Identify the drainage territory you'll practice before beginning.

Hand mechanics

ContactBroad, relaxed hand contact. Avoid poking with fingertips.
StretchTake up skin slack gently in the intended direction rather than sliding across the surface.
ReleaseAllow the skin to return without dragging back with pressure.
RhythmSlow and repetitive, around one deliberate movement per second or slower depending on the movement practiced.
RepetitionsRepeat rhythmic movements in each area before progressing. Quality and direction matter more than mechanically counting.
Body mechanicsKeep wrists and shoulders relaxed. MLD shouldn't require muscular force from the therapist.

Central preparation

Begin with relaxed diaphragmatic breathing and gentle preparation near the supraclavicular/venous-angle region. The purpose isn't to “unclog” a literal drain. It's to begin centrally and prepare proximal drainage before peripheral territories.

Face and neck sequence

StepPractice focus
1Prepare the supraclavicular region and cervical pathways.
2Use gentle neck movements directed toward appropriate cervical drainage.
3Work facial regions in small sections toward relevant preauricular, submandibular/submental and cervical pathways.
4Return through the prepared neck pathway toward central drainage.

Don't use deep pressure over the anterior neck or carotid region. Facial MLD should feel especially light.

Upper trunk and axilla

Prepare the axillary territory before working the arm or superficial upper trunk. Use broad gentle skin stretch toward the axillary region while respecting breast/chest boundaries, consent and draping. Don't reach deeply into the axilla searching for nodes.

Arm sequence

1 · Axilla / proximal shoulderPrepare the proximal drainage territory first.
2 · Upper armBegin proximally, progress farther down, and direct each section back toward the axilla.
3 · Elbow / forearmPrepare proximal forearm territory, progress distally, then direct back proximally.
4 · Wrist / handUse very light work through hand and fingers, then retrace the pathway proximally.

Abdominal practice

Use diaphragmatic breathing and gentle abdominal contact only when screening, consent and comfort support it. Work is superficial and unforced. Avoid abdominal MLD when symptoms, recent surgery, pregnancy-related restrictions or other contraindications make abdominal work inappropriate.

Leg sequence

1 · Inguinal / proximal thighPrepare proximal lower-extremity drainage without invasive contact.
2 · ThighWork by sections, beginning proximally and progressing distally while directing strokes back toward inguinal drainage.
3 · Knee / lower legRespect the popliteal region and continue sectional proximal-to-distal preparation.
4 · Ankle / footFinish distal work lightly, then retrace the pathway proximally toward the groin.

Never interpret calf swelling as a reason to perform drainage. New unilateral warmth, redness, tenderness or swelling requires DVT/referral reasoning.

Posterior body

Identify the posterior midline and transverse drainage concepts before beginning. Upper posterior trunk territories generally direct toward axillary regions, while lower superficial trunk territories direct toward inguinal regions. Work gently and respect watershed boundaries at the introductory level.

Practice the sequence intelligently

Before each region, say out loud: Destination: where does it drain? Preparation: what proximal area comes first? Direction: which way does the skin stretch? Pressure: am I moving skin or compressing muscle? Safety: is anything here a reason to stop?

Common practical errors

ErrorCorrection
Using Swedish pressureReduce depth until movement is primarily superficial skin stretch.
Sliding with lotionReduce lubricant and create controlled traction on the skin.
Starting at hand or footPrepare central and proximal territories first.
Moving everything vaguely “toward the heart”Name the actual drainage territory and follow the anatomical pathway.
RushingSlow down and allow a complete stretch/release cycle.
Pressing deeply into node regionsNodes aren't squeezed like buttons. Keep contact gentle and superficial.
Treating unexplained edemaStop and screen. The cause determines whether MLD is appropriate.
Improvising post-surgical reroutingStay within introductory scope. Rerouting requires specialized training.

Partner lab checkpoints

  1. Can your partner feel skin movement without muscular compression?
  2. Is your rhythm slow and repeatable?
  3. Can you name the drainage territory for every region?
  4. Did you prepare proximal pathways before progressing distally?
  5. Are your hands, wrists and shoulders relaxed?
  6. Can you distinguish a normal tissue observation from a red flag?
  7. Can you explain why new unilateral leg swelling stops the sequence?
  8. Can you explain why post-cancer lymphedema is outside this introductory routine?

Ready for check-off?

You should be able to demonstrate the assigned sequence without relying on a script, explain the anatomy behind your direction, maintain light pressure throughout, recognize when not to proceed, and communicate professionally with your partner.
