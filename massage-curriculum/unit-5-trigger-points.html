Unit 5 · Deep Tissue Technique

Trigger Point Therapy

Learn trigger point therapy as its own focused deep-tissue technique: identify a clinically relevant tender/taut area, reproduce familiar symptoms when appropriate, apply controlled compression, integrate the tissue, and reassess.

Trigger point therapy is not MFR

These are taught as separate deep-tissue approaches. Trigger point therapy centers on focused assessment and pressure to a specific symptomatic point or taut region. Myofascial release centers on sustained engagement with fascial restriction and tissue movement. A therapist may use both during a session, but students should be able to explain and demonstrate each independently.

The trigger point model

A classic myofascial trigger point is described as a hyperirritable spot associated with a hypersensitive palpable nodule in a taut band of skeletal muscle. Trigger points are used clinically to organize patterns of local tenderness, referred symptoms and restricted movement.

Active
Associated with spontaneous or familiar pain and may reproduce the client's complaint when palpated.
Latent
Doesn't produce the client's everyday pain but becomes tender with compression or muscle use and may contribute to stiffness.
Satellite
A secondary trigger point described in the model as developing in response to another symptomatic region.

Keep the science in perspective

Trigger point therapy is widely used, but the biological mechanism and reliability of identifying trigger points by palpation remain debated. Students should understand the model without claiming that every tender knot is a proven lesion or that palpation alone establishes a medical diagnosis. Use the client's familiar symptoms, function, movement, tissue response and reassessment together.

Local pain vs. referred pain

Local tenderness
The client feels the pressure primarily where you're touching. Tenderness alone doesn't automatically make the location a clinically meaningful trigger point.
Referred sensation
The client experiences sensation somewhere away from the contact. Ask whether the referral resembles their familiar complaint.
Don't over-label
Numbness, tingling, weakness, burning or radiating symptoms can have neurological or other causes. Don't automatically call them trigger point referral.
Client recognition matters
“That's the exact pain I get” is more useful than simply finding the most painful spot you can press.

Palpation sequence

Orient to anatomy. Identify the muscle, its fiber direction and nearby endangerment structures before hunting for a point.
Warm the region. Broad massage lets you compare general tissue tone and prepares the area for more focused palpation.
Explore along the muscle. Use slow palpation to notice taut or unusually resistant regions rather than poking randomly.
Cross the fibers. When appropriate, palpate across the suspected taut band to locate a more sensitive nodule/region.
Ask. Is it tender? Does it reproduce the client's familiar symptom? Does it refer somewhere else?
Connect it to function. Compare with relevant movement or ROM instead of treating the palpation finding as an isolated fact.

Manual pressure release

Explain and obtain consent. Focused compression can feel very different from Swedish massage.
Build pressure gradually. Use thumb, supported finger, knuckle, soft fist or elbow depending on the tissue and your training.
Hold steady pressure. Maintain a tolerable sustained contact rather than repeatedly jabbing the point.
Monitor. Pressure should not produce sharp, electrical, alarming or escalating symptoms. Watch for breath holding and guarding.
Ease out gradually. Don't abruptly release a strong contact.
Integrate. Follow focused work with broader massage and, when appropriate, movement or stretching.
Reassess. Compare tenderness, familiar symptoms, ROM or the functional measure that made the point clinically relevant.

Common regions & anatomy connections

Upper trapezius / levator scapulae
Frequently explored with neck and shoulder complaints. Know the scapular and cervical attachments before applying focused pressure.
Scalenes
Require extra caution because important neural and vascular structures are nearby. Upper-extremity neurological symptoms deserve careful reasoning.
Quadratus lumborum
Deep posterior trunk muscle associated with lumbar and pelvic movement. Orient to ribs, iliac crest and lumbar region.
Gluteus medius/minimus
Hip muscles that may be considered when symptoms involve lateral hip or referred lower-extremity patterns.
Forearm flexors/extensors
Useful region for repetitive gripping/wrist complaints, but distinguish muscular tenderness from neurological symptoms.
Gastrocnemius / soleus
Calf work requires vascular screening. Unexplained unilateral swelling, warmth or concerning pain is not a trigger point experiment.
Masseter
External jaw work may be relevant to jaw tension. Stay within training, consent and scope.
Pectoral region
Requires clear consent and draping. Chest/arm symptoms can have causes far beyond muscular trigger points.

Pressure is not the goal

“Therapeutic pain” should never become an excuse to overpower the client's nervous system. A client doesn't need to endure extreme pain for the technique to work. Stop or change approach for sharp, shooting, electrical, burning, numb, rapidly escalating or otherwise concerning symptoms. Guarding, breath holding and pulling away are information too.

Contraindications & modifications

Avoid focused work
Acute injury/inflammation, open wounds, active infection, significant bruising, unstable healing tissue and suspected vascular problems.
Modify intensity
Clients with high sensitivity, chronic pain, altered sensation, easy bruising or conditions associated with flares may require shorter/lighter work.
Endangerment sites
Don't chase a tender point into the anterior neck, axilla, cubital fossa, popliteal fossa, femoral triangle or other vulnerable area.
Reconsider the model
If symptoms don't behave like a muscular complaint, don't keep increasing pressure in an attempt to “find the knot.”

Trigger point vs. MFR

Trigger point therapy
Focused target. Often a taut/tender region. Sustained compression is common. Familiar symptom reproduction and reassessment help determine relevance.
Myofascial release
Broader fascial restriction model. Slow sustained engagement follows tissue resistance. Less dependent on identifying one tender nodule.
Trigger point question
“Does pressure here reproduce a meaningful part of the client's familiar complaint?”
MFR question
“Where does the fascial tissue resist movement, and how does that restriction respond to sustained engagement?”

Connect it to the New Routine

The New Routine teaches students a dependable full-body structure. Trigger point therapy is an intentional focused addition, not permission to stop the flow and hunt for painful spots everywhere.

Back
After broad warming and palpation, a relevant upper-trap or paraspinal region may receive focused work before reconnecting with broader strokes.
Glutes
Piriformis/gluteal work can include focused pressure when indicated, but the named routine step and trigger point assessment aren't automatically the same thing.
Calf
Lobster/strip techniques remain part of the routine. A trigger point approach adds assessment and sustained focused compression only when safe and relevant.
Neck
SCM Cross and Neck Rails don't become “trigger point work” merely because tissue is tender. Technique identity comes from purpose and application.

Lab: find less, reason more

Choose one accessible muscle and state its attachments/action before palpating.
Warm the region and locate a taut/tender area without asking the partner to tolerate excessive pain.
Ask whether pressure produces local tenderness, referral, or the partner's familiar symptom.
Measure one relevant comfortable movement before focused treatment.
Apply gradual sustained compression with appropriate body mechanics.
Integrate with broader massage and/or appropriate stretching.
Reassess the same symptom or movement.
Explain why the technique you just performed is trigger point therapy rather than MFR.

MBLEx + clinical reasoning

What's the key distinction between a latent and active trigger point in the traditional model?

An active point is associated with spontaneous/familiar symptoms, while a latent point generally becomes symptomatic with palpation or use rather than producing the everyday complaint.

Is every tender point a trigger point?

No. Tenderness is nonspecific. Consider tissue findings alongside symptom reproduction, function, movement and the broader clinical picture.

Why reassess after focused pressure?

Reassessment helps determine whether the finding and treatment were clinically meaningful rather than assuming that tissue softness equals success.

A client reports electrical tingling down the arm when you press near the scalenes. Should you increase pressure to release the trigger point?

No. Stop or modify. Neurological symptoms require caution and shouldn't automatically be interpreted as trigger point referral.

Are trigger point therapy and myofascial release the same technique?

No. They may both appear in deep-tissue treatment, but they use different assessment concepts and applications and should be taught as distinct techniques.

What's more useful: finding the most painful spot or reproducing a clinically meaningful familiar symptom?

The clinically meaningful finding. The goal isn't to win a pain contest.
