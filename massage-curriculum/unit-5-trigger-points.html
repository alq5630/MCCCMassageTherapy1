Trigger Point Therapy

Palpate intentionally, recognize familiar symptom patterns, apply focused pressure safely, and reassess instead of treating every tender spot like a trigger point.

Keep the model in perspective

Trigger point theory is widely used in manual therapy, but the exact nature and diagnostic reliability of trigger points remain debated. Students should understand the clinical model without presenting it as a proven tissue diagnosis. A tender point isn't automatically the source of a client's pain.

Three useful categories

Active
Associated with spontaneous or familiar pain and may reproduce symptoms when palpated.
Latent
Tender when compressed but not necessarily producing the client's everyday pain.
Satellite
A secondary tender area described in the trigger point model as developing in response to another symptomatic region.

What you're palpating for

Explore the muscle slowly for a taut or unusually tense region, localized tenderness, and reproduction of a familiar symptom pattern. Compare sides and surrounding tissue. Ask whether the sensation is simply tender or actually resembles the complaint that brought the client in.

Referred pain

Symptoms can be perceived away from the tissue being palpated. Referral patterns can help organize assessment, but they don't prove a diagnosis. Tingling, numbness, weakness, vascular changes, or other neurological symptoms shouldn't automatically be labeled a trigger point referral.

Focused pressure sequence

  1. Warm and assess the broader region.
  2. Locate the most clinically relevant tender/taut area rather than hunting for pain.
  3. Explain the technique and obtain consent.
  4. Apply gradual, tolerable sustained compression.
  5. Monitor for guarding, sharp pain, neurological symptoms, or excessive intensity.
  6. Release gradually, integrate with broader massage or stretching, and reassess.

Common regions

Upper trapezius & levator scapulae
Neck, shoulder, and headache complaints.
Scalenes & pectoral region
Upper-quarter symptoms require careful screening because nerve and vascular conditions can mimic muscular pain.
QL & gluteals
Low-back and hip complaints with possible referral patterns.
Forearm flexors/extensors
Wrist, hand, gripping, and repetitive-use complaints.
Gastrocnemius & soleus
Calf, heel, and foot complaints.
Jaw muscles
Local jaw tension and some headache presentations when within training and scope.

When focused pressure is the wrong tool

Avoid aggressive trigger point work over acute injury or inflammation, infection, open wounds, bruising, unstable tissue, suspected clotting problems, or areas where sensation is impaired. Modify for chronic pain conditions and stop for sharp, shooting, electrical, or escalating symptoms.
