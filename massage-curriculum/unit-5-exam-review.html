Theory Exam #5 Review

Use this as an active study guide. Theory #5 is entirely ethics and the therapeutic relationship. Trigger point therapy, MFR, deep tissue and MLD do not belong on this exam.

Exam map

50 questions: ethical principles and professional conduct; consent and client rights; boundaries and dual relationships; confidentiality and reporting; transference/countertransference; communication and cultural humility; sexual boundaries; competence, referral, records, advertising and scenario-based ethical decisions.

1. Seven principles

Autonomy
The client controls decisions about their body and treatment. Consent can be limited or withdrawn.
Beneficence
Choose actions intended to benefit the client.
Nonmaleficence
Avoid preventable harm. Modify, postpone or refer when risk outweighs benefit.
Veracity
Be truthful about credentials, claims, evidence, risks and services.
Fidelity
Honor professional commitments and remain dependable.
Justice
Practice fairly and without unjust discrimination.
Confidentiality
Protect private information within legal/professional limits.

Exam trick: several principles may seem relevant. Choose the one most directly described by the stem. Refusing unwanted touch is primarily autonomy. False advertising is primarily veracity. Performing a dangerous technique despite a contraindication points toward nonmaleficence.

2. Consent & client rights

  • Consent is informed, voluntary and ongoing.
  • A signed intake form is not permanent blanket permission for every body area or technique.
  • A client can change their mind at any time, including during treatment.
  • Explain sensitive-area work before touching and obtain appropriate consent.
  • The therapist is responsible for secure draping and professional boundaries.
  • If the client asks to stop, stop. They don't need to justify the decision.

3. Boundary map

Physical
Touch, draping, positioning, undress, privacy and sensitive areas.
Emotional
Avoid rescue, dependency and making the client care for the therapist.
Time
Predictable starts, endings and scheduling policies.
Financial
Fees, gifts, discounts, bartering, tips and product sales.
Intellectual
Don't impose personal beliefs or ideology.
Digital
Texting, social media, photographs, privacy and online role clarity.
Location/social
Maintain professional role clarity when encountering clients outside treatment.

Boundary crossing: a departure from usual boundaries that may be benign or appropriate in context. Boundary violation: exploitation or conduct that harms the client or places the therapist's needs above the client's welfare.

4. Power differential & sexual boundaries

The therapist has professional knowledge, role authority and access to a client who may be undressed, physically vulnerable or sharing private information. That power differential creates greater responsibility for the therapist.

  • Client initiation doesn't erase the therapist's responsibility.
  • Sexualized touch is outside the professional massage relationship.
  • Repeated sexual comments can justify stopping and terminating a session.
  • An involuntary physiological response such as an erection isn't automatically misconduct. Respond to behavior and context.
  • Accidental exposure should be corrected promptly while restoring the client's security and dignity.

5. Transference, countertransference & self-disclosure

Transference = client → therapist. The client brings earlier feelings, expectations or relationship patterns into the professional relationship.

Countertransference = therapist reaction. Warning signs include rescuing, favoritism, jealousy, bending rules, repeated extra time, excessive thoughts about one client, unusual defensiveness or feeling personally responsible for fixing the client's life.

Before self-disclosing, ask: Does this help the client, or am I asking the client to meet my emotional need?

6. Confidentiality

  • Don't casually confirm that someone is a client.
  • Don't discuss clients where others can overhear.
  • Protect written and electronic records.
  • Don't disclose session information to spouses, friends or relatives simply because they ask.
  • Educational discussion still requires appropriate privacy/de-identification/authorization practices.
  • Don't promise absolute secrecy because laws may create reporting or disclosure exceptions.

7. Communication & cultural humility

Active listening includes attending, clarifying and reflecting without taking over. Ask respectful questions when language is unclear rather than assuming meaning.

Cultural humility is ongoing self-reflection and respectful curiosity. Don't memorize stereotypes. Collaborate on safe modifications to draping, communication or treatment when they matter to the individual client.

8. Emotional responses

If a client cries, laughs, becomes quiet or has another emotional response, stay calm, check in and let the client choose whether to pause or continue. Don't diagnose trauma, interpret why they're emotional or turn massage into psychotherapy.

9. Scope, competence & referral

  • Massage therapists don't diagnose disease.
  • Don't prescribe medications or medical treatment outside scope.
  • Stay within training and competence even when a client requests something else.
  • Refer when the client's needs exceed massage scope or your competence.
  • Accurate credentials and truthful advertising are ethical obligations.

10. Records, gifts & financial relationships

Documentation should be accurate, relevant, timely and professional. Gifts and bartering aren't solved by one automatic rule in every situation. Consider value, intent, practice policy, role clarity, power dynamics and whether the arrangement could influence professional judgment.

Ethical decision algorithm

  1. Identify the actual problem and separate facts from assumptions.
  2. Identify client rights, safety issues and competing ethical duties.
  3. Check applicable law, regulation, scope, policy and professional standards.
  4. Consider reasonable options and likely consequences.
  5. Consult an appropriate supervisor, board resource, insurer or legal resource when needed.
  6. Choose the action that best protects client welfare and professional obligations.
  7. Document objectively when the situation requires documentation.

Scenario drill

A client refuses neck work you think would help.

Respect the refusal. Autonomy controls. Offer alternatives if appropriate without pressuring the client.

A client cries during massage.

Pause or check in, allow choice, maintain presence and don't interpret or counsel.

A client offers a very expensive gift.

Consider value, intent, policy, power dynamics and the effect on the professional relationship before accepting.

A client sends a social-media friend request.

Consider privacy, confidentiality, role clarity, dual-relationship risk and practice policy rather than automatically accepting.

You notice you're giving one client extra time every visit.

Examine possible countertransference or overinvolvement and restore consistent boundaries.

A client makes repeated sexual comments after redirection.

Clearly reassert the boundary and terminate the session if the behavior continues or safety requires it.

A client asks you to diagnose numbness.

Stay within scope. Describe relevant observations and recommend appropriate evaluation rather than diagnosing.

A client asks you to keep something secret that triggers a legal reporting duty.

Explain the limits of confidentiality and follow applicable law and required procedure.

Last-minute traps

Client initiated ≠ automatically ethical. Signed form ≠ permanent consent. Empathy ≠ becoming the client's counselor. Equal treatment ≠ ignoring individual cultural needs. Deep friendship ≠ stronger therapeutic relationship. Good intentions ≠ permission to work outside scope. Confidentiality ≠ absolute secrecy.
