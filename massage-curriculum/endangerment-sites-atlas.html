Endangerment Sites Atlas

A regional map of vulnerable anatomy, why it matters, what warning sensations mean, and how to modify massage safely.

What is an endangerment site?

An endangerment or cautionary site is an area where careless pressure can irritate or injure vulnerable structures such as nerves, vessels, organs, lymph nodes or bony prominences. It does not automatically mean “never touch.” Use anatomical precision, broad/light contact when appropriate, and advanced training for deeper work.

General tissue rules

StructureMain concernMassage reasoning
NerveCompression, pinning or stretch may cause sharp, shooting, burning, tingling, numb or electric symptoms.Neurological symptoms mean change or stop the contact. Do not teach clients that nerve pain is “good pain.”
ArterySustained compression can reduce blood flow; some arterial regions have additional reflex/vascular risks.A palpable pulse is a clue that focused sustained compression is inappropriate.
VeinSuperficial vessels can be vulnerable, especially with varicosities, inflammation or thrombotic risk.Avoid aggressive direct pressure over compromised veins.
Lymph nodeEnlargement/tenderness can reflect immune activation or disease.Do not aggressively manipulate enlarged, hard or painful nodes.
OrganSome organs become more vulnerable when enlarged, inflamed, displaced or diseased.Deep abdominal work requires history, anatomy and appropriate scope/training.
Bony prominenceSoft tissue may be pinched or ground against bone; compromised bone is more vulnerable.Work around rather than driving tissue into sharp/hard landmarks.

Head & face

Eyes: direct pressure is inappropriate. Consider comfort with contact lenses and prone positioning.
Trigeminal nerve exits: supraorbital, infraorbital and mental branches emerge through foramina. Heavy focal pressure can be painful and irritate superficial nerves.
Retromandibular region: the facial nerve exits near the ear/mastoid neighborhood. Precise anatomy matters during jaw and SCM-adjacent work.
Styloid process: a small pointed temporal-bone projection medial to the mastoid. It is not a target for deep pressure.
Greater occipital nerves: emerge in the posterior occipital region. Avoid prolonged, irritating focal compression when symptoms are reproduced.

Anterior neck

This region contains major vascular, airway, endocrine and lymphatic structures. Advanced anatomical knowledge is required for specific work.

Common carotid artery/carotid sinus: do not apply sustained or forceful direct pressure. Carotid sinus stimulation can cause marked cardiovascular responses, dizziness or faintness.
Jugular veins: avoid direct compression/manipulation.
Hyoid: small mobile U-shaped bone involved with swallowing/speech musculature. Do not compress it.
Thyroid cartilage and trachea: anterior airway structures are not massage targets. Compression can cause pain/choking sensation.
Thyroid gland: do not attempt to manipulate the gland to change hormone function. That is outside massage scope.
Cervical lymph nodes: enlarged, hard or painful nodes warrant local avoidance and appropriate follow-up.
Esophagus: deep to the airway and not a massage target.

New Routine connection: SCM Cross and Neck Rails require precise contact. More pressure is not better pressure here.

Lateral neck / posterior triangle

External jugular vein: crosses superficially over SCM.
Cervical transverse processes: avoid pinning scalene tissue into sharp bony landmarks.
Brachial plexus roots: neurological symptoms with pressure require immediate modification.
Spinal accessory nerve: vulnerable in the posterior triangle; it supplies SCM and trapezius.

Chest & infraclavicular region

Brachial plexus and subclavian/axillary vessels: vulnerable structures pass under/around the clavicular and axillary regions. Use broad contact rather than point pressure when working near them.
Cephalic vein: can be superficial near the deltopectoral groove.
Breast tissue: sensitive tissue requiring clear boundaries, draping and consent. Specialized breast massage requires appropriate training and legal scope.

New Routine connection: Pec Glide and V-Stroke Flow stay anatomically intentional and consent-driven.

Abdomen

Xiphoid process: small inferior sternal projection. Never drive sharp downward pressure onto it.
Floating ribs: avoid pinning tissue against the free rib ends.
Liver/gallbladder: healthy organs are relatively protected, but enlargement/inflammation changes tolerance and safety.
Spleen: normally protected under left ribs; enlargement, including with some illnesses, increases vulnerability.
Intestines: abdominal massage is not appropriate for suspected obstruction, impaction, acute inflammatory abdominal conditions or unexplained severe symptoms.
Pelvic organs: pathology, surgery, pregnancy or inflammation can alter location/sensitivity and require modification.
Abdominal aorta: do not use sustained deep compression over a prominent pulsatile abdominal mass. Unexpected prominent pulsation warrants medical evaluation rather than massage experimentation.

Posterior trunk

Kidney region: kidneys are retroperitoneal and partly protected by lower ribs and substantial tissue. Avoid aggressive pounding/deep intrusive work, especially with known renal pathology or tenderness.
Spinous processes: avoid grinding paraspinal tissue into vertebral processes.

Routine connection: Erector Glide, QL Sweep and back work should target soft tissue, not prove how hard the therapist can press.

Upper extremity

Axilla: contains brachial plexus branches, axillary artery/vein and lymph nodes. Specific work requires skill and consent.
Radial nerve: travels around posterior humerus and can be irritated by focal pressure.
Medial upper arm: multiple nerves and superficial vessels can be vulnerable.
Antecubital fossa: contains major vessels and median/radial nerve structures. Avoid deep direct compression.
Cubital tunnel: ulnar nerve lies behind medial epicondyle. “Funny bone” electrical symptoms are nerve irritation, not a trigger point.
Carpal tunnel: median nerve symptoms can be aggravated by direct irritating pressure when the nerve is sensitized.

Routine connection: Forearm Strip and Arm Knead must follow tissue rather than compressing vulnerable pits/grooves.

Lower extremity

Femoral triangle: bordered by inguinal ligament, sartorius and adductor longus. Contains femoral nerve, artery, vein and nearby lymphatics. Deep medial-thigh work requires strict boundaries and anatomical precision.
Deep gluteal/sciatic region: sciatic nerve is large and well protected but irritated neural tissue can reproduce radiating symptoms. Do not chase nerve symptoms with deeper pressure.
Popliteal fossa: contains popliteal artery/vein and tibial/common fibular nerve structures. No indiscriminate deep pressure in the knee pit.
Great saphenous vein: superficial along medial leg and commonly involved in varicosities. Compromised veins require modification.
Small saphenous vein: travels posterior calf toward popliteal region and may also develop varicosities.
Common fibular nerve: particularly superficial near fibular head. Shock-like symptoms mean back off immediately.
Tarsal tunnel: posterior tibial nerve and vessels pass behind medial malleolus; symptomatic nerve tissue should not be aggressively compressed.

Routine connection: Adductor Wring, Ham Glide, calf work, Lobster Shin and Shin Strip all have nearby structures that determine where pressure belongs.

Stop signs during contact

Unexpected sharp, shooting, burning, electric or radiating pain; new numbness/tingling; dizziness/faintness; breathing difficulty; severe unexplained pain; rapidly increasing swelling; or a client asking you to stop. These are not invitations to “work through it.”

Core exam distinction

Endangerment site: anatomy makes the region vulnerable and technique must be modified. Local contraindication: avoid a specific area while massage elsewhere may still be appropriate. Systemic/absolute contraindication: postpone the session because massage is not appropriate at that time. Needs more information: pause the plan until risk is clarified.
