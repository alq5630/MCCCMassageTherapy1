Special Populations & Prenatal Massage

Adapt the massage to the person in front of you. Life stage, pregnancy, age, disability, occupation, medical history and sensory needs may change positioning, consent, pressure, duration, communication and risk screening without reducing the client to a label.

Special population does not mean “fragile person”

The clinical skill is identifying the specific reason a modification is useful. Ask what the individual needs, screen relevant risks, explain choices and preserve autonomy. Don't use age, pregnancy, disability or diagnosis as a shortcut for assumptions.

Prenatal massage by stage

First trimester

Pregnancy itself is not an automatic contraindication. Screen for concerning symptoms. Cramping with vaginal bleeding or symptoms suggesting ectopic pregnancy/miscarriage require medical evaluation rather than routine massage. Also screen the legs for concerning unilateral swelling, heat, redness or pain.

Comfort: nausea may make semi-reclined or upright positioning preferable. Breast tenderness can require extra cushioning and positioning changes.

Second trimester

As the uterus enlarges, prolonged flat supine positioning may become uncomfortable or contribute to aortocaval compression in some clients. Side-lying, semi-reclining or a leftward tilt can improve comfort and circulation.

Important screening: concerning widespread edema, severe headache, visual changes, significant blood-pressure concerns or a diagnosed pregnancy complication require appropriate obstetric guidance rather than routine massage assumptions.

Third trimester

Support joints carefully, use comfortable available ROM and avoid forcing traction or mobility. Reflux may favor a more upright position. Mild dependent ankle/foot swelling is different from unilateral hot painful swelling or sudden widespread edema, which changes the safety decision.

Postpartum

Delivery method, surgical healing, bleeding, fatigue, breast/chest comfort, clot risk, medications and positioning all matter. A postpartum client is not simply “no longer pregnant.” After Cesarean delivery, protect healing abdominal tissues and use positions that do not compress the incision.

Prenatal positioning lab

Side-lying
Support the head, upper arm and upper leg. Keep the spine neutral and the client stable near enough to the table edge for therapist access.
Semi-reclined
Useful when lying flat worsens reflux, nausea, breathing or late-pregnancy comfort.
Left lateral tilt
A small support under the right hip can create a leftward tilt when a pregnant client is otherwise positioned supine.
Seated
Useful when table positioning is uncomfortable, impractical or poorly tolerated.

DVT and pregnancy: don't massage the suspicious calf

Pregnancy and the postpartum period can increase thromboembolic risk. New unilateral leg swelling, localized heat, redness, tenderness or pain is not an invitation to “work out the calf.” Stop and seek appropriate medical evaluation. Students should connect this directly to Calf Effleurage, Calf Split, Lobster Claw and every other lower-leg technique in the New Routine.

Pregnancy + New Routine modifications

Prone back routine
Positioning may need side-lying or another supported alternative as pregnancy progresses. Don't force the standard table setup.
Glute/posterior leg
Adapt access and draping to side-lying when needed. Screen carefully for vascular concerns before leg work.
Heel Hold / traction
Use conservative, supported movement rather than aggressive lower-extremity traction.
Supine anterior work
Modify prolonged flat supine positioning later in pregnancy according to comfort and current prenatal guidance.
Neck/chest work
Bolster for breast/chest tenderness, reflux and breathing comfort. Maintain explicit consent and boundaries.
Transitions
Allow more time for position changes and getting off the table. Dizziness and balance can alter transfer safety.

Infants, children and adolescents

Infant massage

Your course material emphasizes teaching parents/caregivers to provide infant massage rather than simply treating the infant as an adult client in miniature. The caregiver learns to notice engagement and disengagement cues, keep sessions short, maintain warmth and use techniques the child tolerates and enjoys.

Children and adolescents

Guardian permission does not erase the young person's assent. If a child does not want massage, massage should not proceed. Explain procedures in age-appropriate language, use clear safeguards and preserve privacy without creating secrecy.

Session length, clothing/draping, environment and technique should be developmentally appropriate. A familiar object or caregiver presence may help a younger child feel secure.

Older adults

Aging is highly individual. Common considerations can include thinner or more fragile skin, reduced bone density and muscle mass, sensory changes, slower transfers, chronic conditions, polypharmacy and orthostatic hypotension. None of those justify assuming every older adult needs feather-light massage.

Skin
Observe tissue fragility, bruising and healing. Adjust friction and pressure accordingly.
Bone/joint health
Osteoporosis, arthritis and joint replacements can change bolstering, pressure and ROM.
Transfers
Allow enough time for sitting, standing and changing positions. Don't rush a dizzy client.
Communication
Account for hearing or vision changes without speaking to the client as though they are a child.

Tactical athletes, military and first responders

Job demands can involve heavy equipment, repetitive loading, sudden physical exertion, old injuries, hearing changes, sleep disruption and high stress. Ask about the client's actual duties rather than assuming what their body has experienced.

Be thoughtful about the sensory environment. Loud noises, unexpected door openings or sudden touch can provoke a startle response in some clients. Explain transitions and preserve client control without assuming every veteran or first responder has PTSD.

Disability and chronic illness

Ask what assistance is wanted before providing it. A wheelchair, prosthesis, mobility device, sensory difference or chronic diagnosis does not tell you the client's pressure preference, cognitive ability or goals. Modify table access, bolstering, communication, temperature, session length and techniques based on the individual.

Adapt the routine lab

Students receive a client profile and must redesign the New Routine without losing professionalism or flow.

Identify which parts of the standard table positioning need to change.
Choose bolsters and explain why each is used.
Identify at least three New Routine techniques that need modification, relocation or omission.
Explain one risk that must be screened before treatment.
Demonstrate how you would ask for consent without treating the client as fragile.
Perform a five-minute adapted sequence.
Reassess comfort and function before the client changes position or stands.

Scenario rounds

Pregnancy
A client at 30 weeks becomes lightheaded while lying flat. What do you change immediately?
Minor
A guardian wants the massage to continue, but the 12-year-old says they want to stop. Whose signal controls the massage?
Older adult
An active 72-year-old takes an anticoagulant and has fragile bruised skin. Which factor, not age alone, should drive pressure decisions?
Tactical athlete
A firefighter with old shoulder injuries startles when touched without warning. How can communication and environment change the session?

MBLEx-style reasoning

A pregnant client has new unilateral calf swelling, warmth and tenderness. What is the best massage decision?

Do not massage the affected leg. The presentation requires prompt medical evaluation for a possible vascular problem.

A guardian consents to massage but the child refuses. Should massage proceed?

No. The child's assent and willingness matter. A resisting or unwilling minor should not be massaged simply because a guardian signed consent.

Does every older adult require very light massage?

No. Assess the individual. Tissue integrity, bone health, medications, sensation, conditions, goals and tolerance guide pressure.

Why might late pregnancy change supine positioning?

The enlarged uterus can contribute to aortocaval compression and discomfort in some clients. Side-lying, semi-reclined positioning or an appropriate tilt can be used.

What is the best first step when a client with a disability arrives with a caregiver?

Address the client directly and ask what assistance or adaptations they want rather than making assumptions.
