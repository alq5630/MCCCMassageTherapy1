Block 3 · Medical Massage

Special Populations & Prenatal Massage

Special-population massage is clinical adaptation, not a separate set of universal rules. Students learn to screen the individual, identify vulnerabilities and strengths, and modify positioning, pressure, duration, communication and environment without stereotyping the client.

The central rule: individualize

Age, pregnancy, disability or diagnosis alone doesn't tell you whether massage is safe. Ask what is happening with this person today. Consider health status, medications, sensation, skin integrity, mobility, cognition, communication, fatigue, medical devices, recent procedures and the client's own preferences.

Pregnancy: normal changes that affect massage

Cardiovascular
Blood volume and cardiac workload increase. Pregnancy also changes venous return and clot risk, so new unilateral leg symptoms deserve careful screening.
Musculoskeletal
Changing posture, altered center of gravity and increased load may contribute to back, hip, pelvic and leg discomfort. Support joints rather than forcing range.
Respiratory/GI
Shortness of breath, reflux and abdominal pressure may make semireclined or side-lying positioning more comfortable.
Edema
Dependent swelling can occur, especially later in pregnancy. Sudden, severe, unilateral or otherwise concerning swelling isn't something to simply massage away.

Prenatal red flags

Massage therapists aren't diagnosing obstetric complications, but they should recognize symptoms that need medical attention. Examples include unexplained vaginal bleeding, severe headache or visual changes, sudden or concerning swelling, chest pain, shortness of breath beyond expected exertional changes, signs of possible DVT, severe abdominal/pelvic pain, fever or other acute illness, and any complication for which the client's healthcare team has placed restrictions.

MBLEx reasoning: pregnancy isn't itself a contraindication. The complication or presentation determines whether massage proceeds, is modified, or is postponed/referred.

Postpartum massage

The postpartum period varies dramatically. Ask about vaginal versus cesarean delivery, complications, bleeding, incisions, pain, infection, blood-clot concerns, medications, sleep deprivation and feeding/positioning needs. Avoid direct work over unhealed surgical tissue. Comfortable side-lying, semireclined or prone positioning may be chosen based on healing and breast/chest comfort.

Infants and young children

Consent + assent
A parent/guardian may provide legal permission, but the child's behavior and willingness matter. Stop when a child withdraws assent.
Shorter dosage
Sessions and techniques are generally shorter and adapted to size, development and attention.
Communication
Explain touch in simple language. Don't surprise the child. Offer choices when possible.
Environment
Follow school/workplace policies for minors, guardian presence and documentation. Maintain professional draping and boundaries.

Adolescents

Teen clients deserve privacy, respect and bodily autonomy while the therapist also follows applicable consent laws and facility policies. Explain the treatment plan directly to the adolescent, obtain assent, avoid talking only to the parent, and maintain especially clear boundaries and draping. A guardian's request doesn't override a client's refusal of touch.

Older adults

Aging isn't a disease. Healthy older adults may tolerate massage very well, while others need significant modification. Screen for osteoporosis, fragile skin, easy bruising, anticoagulants, joint replacements, reduced sensation, edema, cardiovascular disease, balance issues, cognitive changes and difficulty transferring on or off the table.

Pressure
Match tissue quality and medication effects. Thin or fragile tissue may require broader, lighter contact.
Positioning
Use bolsters and allow extra transition time. Semireclined or side-lying may be easier than prone/supine.
Temperature
Monitor warmth carefully. Reduced sensation or circulation can make thermal modalities less appropriate.
Communication
Don't infantilize. Address the client directly even when a caregiver is present.

Clients with disabilities

Don't assume what a person can or can't do. Ask what assistance is wanted before helping with a wheelchair, transfer, prosthesis, mobility device or clothing. Mobility equipment is part of the client's personal space. Adapt the treatment environment when possible instead of requiring the client to adapt to a standard routine.

ConsiderationClinical adaptation
Limited mobilityMassage may be performed in a wheelchair or other supported position when transfer isn't necessary or desired.
Altered sensationUse conservative pressure/temperature and visual skin checks because feedback may be reduced.
Spasticity or involuntary movementDon't force limbs into a “normal” position. Support comfortable range and work with the nervous system rather than against it.
ProsthesisAsk about removal, comfort, skin integrity and sensitivity of the residual limb. Never assume.
Communication disabilityUse the client's preferred communication method and allow time. Don't equate speech difficulty with impaired cognition.

Sensory and neurodivergent clients

Massage can involve intense sensory input. Ask about preferences for lighting, sound, conversation, scent, pressure, predictability and transitions. Explain before changing body regions or techniques. A consistent sequence may help some clients, while others prefer more control over pacing. Avoid framing sensory needs as misbehavior or noncompliance.

Clients with chronic illness and fatigue

For conditions associated with fluctuating fatigue, pain, dizziness or exercise intolerance, today's baseline matters. Shorter sessions, slower transitions, positioning changes and reduced intensity may be more useful than a standard hour-long routine. A client who tolerated a technique last month may need something different during a flare.

Oncology massage principles

Cancer isn't an automatic contraindication to massage, and massage doesn't “spread cancer” through ordinary circulation. The treatment plan is based on the client's current medical status and cancer-treatment effects. Consider surgery and incisions, radiation-related skin changes, ports/lines, bone fragility or metastasis, neuropathy, fatigue, nausea, infection risk, platelet/bleeding concerns and lymphedema risk. Oncology massage may require specialized training and coordination with the healthcare team.

Clients with diabetes

Ask about sensation, skin integrity, circulation concerns, glucose-management routines and medication timing when relevant. Peripheral neuropathy can reduce the client's ability to judge pressure or temperature. Inspect the treatment area appropriately, avoid aggressive work on compromised tissue and know how your facility handles signs of hypoglycemia or other acute problems.

Athletes aren't automatically candidates for deep work

Athletic clients still require pathology screening. Training soreness, acute injury, dehydration, heat illness, concussion symptoms, recent competition and recovery goals all change the plan. Pre-event work generally differs in pace and intent from post-event or recovery massage. Don't use pain tolerance as permission for tissue damage.

Trauma-informed adaptation

Trauma history may or may not be disclosed. Good massage practice reduces unnecessary loss of control for everyone: explain what you're doing, ask rather than assume, provide choices, respect a changed mind, use secure draping and avoid interpreting a freeze response or silence as enthusiastic consent. Trauma-informed care doesn't mean acting as a psychotherapist. Stay in massage scope.

Special-population exam strategy

When a question identifies a population, don't immediately choose “contraindicated.” Ask what specific risk is present. Then choose the least restrictive safe response: normal massage, modification, local avoidance, referral/clearance or postponement. The diagnosis or life stage is context. The client's current presentation drives the decision.

Practice cases

  1. Pregnant client with new unilateral calf warmth and swelling: don't massage the leg. Refer for prompt medical evaluation because of possible DVT.
  2. Older client taking an anticoagulant with easy bruising: don't assume all massage is prohibited. Reduce aggressive pressure and account for bleeding/bruising risk.
  3. Wheelchair user who declines transfer: adapt massage to a safe supported position rather than insisting on the table.
  4. Child pulls away and says “stop” while the parent says to continue: stop. Guardian permission doesn't eliminate the child's assent.
  5. Client receiving cancer treatment reports new fever: postpone massage and direct the client toward appropriate medical guidance rather than treating through systemic illness.
  6. Client with neuropathy asks for very hot stones on numb feet: choose a safer approach because impaired sensation reduces reliable thermal feedback.

Mastery check

You should be able to explain how pregnancy, age, disability, chronic illness, cancer treatment, altered sensation and developmental stage can affect massage without turning any population into a blanket contraindication. For Theory Exam #4, expect the safest answer to depend on the individual's current risks, medications, symptoms and ability to give meaningful feedback.
