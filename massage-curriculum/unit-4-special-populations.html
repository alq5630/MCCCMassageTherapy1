Special Populations & Prenatal Massage

The massage principles don't disappear when a client's needs change. Positioning, consent, pressure, duration, communication, and risk screening become more individualized.

What makes a population "special"?

The term refers to groups who may have additional vulnerability, risk, developmental considerations, physical limitations, or life-stage needs. The goal isn't to treat someone as fragile by default. It's to identify the specific modification that lets massage remain safe, respectful, and useful.

Pregnancy isn't automatically a contraindication

Massage can be appropriate during a healthy pregnancy. Screen for complications and modify positioning and technique as pregnancy progresses. Vaginal bleeding, signs of DVT, diagnosed preeclampsia, concerning widespread edema, or other significant complications require appropriate medical evaluation rather than routine prenatal massage.

Prenatal positioning

Side-lying
Support the head, upper arm, and upper leg. Keep the spine comfortable and stable.
Semireclined
Useful for comfort, reflux, breathing, and avoiding prolonged flat supine positioning later in pregnancy.
Left lateral tilt
A cushion under the right hip can tilt a supine client toward the left when appropriate.
Seated
A useful option when table positioning is uncomfortable or impractical.

Pregnancy-specific reasoning

Watch for unilateral leg swelling, heat, redness, or pain suggesting possible DVT. Later pregnancy may require more joint support and less aggressive mobilization because connective tissues change. Mild dependent swelling may be managed conservatively when otherwise uncomplicated, while unilateral or widespread swelling warrants a different response. Postpartum massage also needs consideration of delivery method, healing tissues, clot risk, positioning, and client comfort.

Children, adolescents & consent

A parent or legal guardian's permission doesn't erase the child's assent. A child who doesn't want massage shouldn't be massaged. Use developmentally appropriate language, preserve privacy, allow familiar comfort objects when helpful, and maintain clear professional safeguards in the treatment environment.

Adapt, don't stereotype

Older adults, people with disabilities, tactical athletes, military members, people living with chronic illness, and other populations aren't one-size-fits-all groups. Ask what the individual needs. Modify bolstering, transfers, session length, pressure, communication, sensory environment, and technique based on the person in front of you.
