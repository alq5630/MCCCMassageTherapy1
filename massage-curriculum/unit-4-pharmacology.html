Pharmacology & Massage Therapy

Massage therapists don't prescribe, recommend stopping medication or practice pharmacy. They do need to recognize when a medication's therapeutic effects, side effects, delivery method or reason for use changes today's massage plan.

The question isn't “What drug is this?”

The better massage question is: What does this medication or its underlying condition change about pressure, sensation, bruising, alertness, blood pressure, positioning, heat, movement, skin integrity, timing or emergency planning?

Core language

Pharmacology
Study of drugs, their actions, uses, dosages, therapeutic effects and adverse effects.
Pharmacotherapy
Using drugs to treat or manage disease.
Pharmacokinetics
What the body does to a drug: absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion.
Pharmacodynamics
What a drug does to the body through its physiological and receptor-level effects.
Therapeutic effect
The intended action. Even intended effects can matter to massage, such as reduced pain sensation.
Side/adverse effect
An unintended effect that may require a modification, such as dizziness, bruising or drowsiness.

Routes of administration

Oral
Tablets, capsules and liquids are common. Effects can still change alertness, sensation, blood pressure and other massage variables.
Injection
Know where injections occur and avoid treating a recent, irritated, bruised or medically restricted injection site.
Transdermal/topical
Do not massage directly over medication patches. Lubricant can interfere with adhesives and direct contact may alter or contaminate the delivery site.
Inhaled/mucosal
A medication doesn't have to enter through the GI tract to have systemic effects.
Infusion/pump
Protect tubing, ports and devices from pressure, traction and lubricant. Follow client/provider restrictions.
Supplements + OTC
They still count. Supplements and nonprescription drugs can affect bruising, sedation and other treatment variables.

Medication intake that actually helps

Ask what the client takes, why they take it, route of administration, relevant timing, recent changes and whether they notice side effects. Don't tell a client to stop or change a medication. If you need medication-specific information, use a reliable drug reference or ask the client to clarify instructions with their pharmacist or prescriber.

High-yield medication categories

CategoryMassage-relevant thinking
NSAIDsPain and inflammation may be reduced, so symptoms can be partially masked. Some clients report GI distress or easier bruising.
Long-term corticosteroidsSkin/connective tissue fragility, bruising, bone-density concerns and other systemic effects can change pressure and positioning.
Muscle relaxants / sedating pain medicationDrowsiness, dizziness and altered sensation can affect pressure feedback, transfers and post-session safety.
Diabetes medications / insulinKnow signs of hypoglycemia. Injection or pump sites need protection. Session timing, meals and symptoms matter.
Anticoagulants / antiplateletsReduced clotting increases bruising/bleeding concerns. Avoid assuming a client can tolerate prolonged deep focused pressure simply because they request it.
Antihypertensives / vasodilatorsDizziness and orthostatic hypotension may require slow position changes and extra time sitting before standing.
DiureticsFrequent urination is an expected effect. Dizziness, weakness or cramping may also affect the session.
Lipid-lowering drugsSome clients report muscle pain or weakness. Don't automatically label medication-related muscle symptoms as a “knot.”
Antihistamines and respiratory medicationsDryness, GI discomfort, dizziness or sedation may influence positioning and comfort. The respiratory condition itself may also matter.

Medication can hide information you normally use

Pain-relieving, sedating or sensation-altering medications can make “Does this pressure hurt?” less reliable as your only safety check. Combine client feedback with health history, tissue response, visual observation, anatomical knowledge and conservative dosing. Never use reduced pain perception as permission to work harder.

New Routine medication checkpoints

Elbow Melt / Target Points
Anticoagulant use, tissue fragility or reduced sensation may require much less pressure or a different technique.
Leg work
Medication doesn't rule out vascular risk. New unilateral calf swelling, heat or pain remains a stop-and-refer concern.
Finger Weave / Neck Stretch
Dizziness, sedation or neurological symptoms change movement and traction decisions.
Pec Glide / Clavicle Circles
Check for ports, patches, devices and surgical history before working through the region.
Position changes
Clients taking medications associated with orthostatic hypotension may need slow transitions and seated recovery time.
Heat or cold
Altered sensation, circulation concerns and certain medication effects can make thermal applications less appropriate.

Medication detective lab

For each mock intake, students do not diagnose or recommend medication changes. They identify the massage implications.

Identify why the medication is being used.
Identify the route: oral, injection, topical/transdermal, inhaled or device.
Name one therapeutic effect that could matter to massage.
Name one side effect that could matter to massage.
Choose one New Routine technique that may require modification.
Explain pressure, positioning or transfer changes.
State what additional information you would seek from a reliable source.

Emergency thinking: hypoglycemia

Your course material identifies confusion/disorientation, tremors, seizures and loss of consciousness as concerning signs of significant hypoglycemia. A conscious client with known diabetes who recognizes low blood sugar may follow their established glucose-treatment plan. A loss of consciousness or other severe emergency requires emergency response. Massage stops while the emergency is addressed.

MBLEx-style reasoning

A client takes an anticoagulant and asks for prolonged elbow pressure because they “like it painful.” What matters most?

The increased bruising/bleeding concern. Pressure should be modified based on medication effects and tissue safety rather than the client's belief that pain makes treatment more effective.

A client becomes dizzy when sitting up after massage and takes an antihypertensive. What is an appropriate response?

Have the client remain safely seated, allow time for symptoms to settle and avoid rushing them to stand. Orthostatic hypotension is a relevant medication effect.

Why ask about supplements and OTC drugs?

Because nonprescription products can still have physiological effects and interactions relevant to massage planning.

A medication patch is located where you planned to perform friction. What should you do?

Avoid direct massage and lubricant over the patch and modify the local treatment plan.

Does knowing a drug name allow a massage therapist to tell the client to stop taking it?

No. Medication prescribing and discontinuation are outside massage therapy scope.
