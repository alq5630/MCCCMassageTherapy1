Block 3 · Medical Massage

Pharmacology & Massage Therapy

Massage therapists don't prescribe, recommend discontinuing, or adjust medications. They do need enough pharmacology to recognize how medications can alter pain, sensation, bruising, alertness, blood pressure, skin integrity, immune function and treatment planning.

The massage question isn't “What does this drug treat?”

The better questions are: Why is this client taking it? What relevant effects or side effects might it produce? Does its route create an area I need to avoid? Could it mask symptoms or change the client's ability to give feedback? What modification, if any, makes today's massage safer?

Core pharmacology language

Drug
A substance used to prevent, diagnose, treat or manage disease or symptoms.
Pharmacology
The study of drugs, including mechanisms, effects and use.
Pharmacotherapy
Use of medications to treat or manage disease.
Prescription (Rx)
An authorized order for a medication or treatment from a qualified prescriber.
Generic name
The nonproprietary drug name, such as ibuprofen.
Trade/brand name
A manufacturer's marketed name, such as Advil or Motrin.
OTC
Medication available without a prescription. OTC doesn't mean risk-free.
Controlled substance
A drug regulated because of abuse/dependence potential. Massage therapists don't need to memorize every schedule to make massage decisions.

Pharmacokinetics: what the body does to the drug

A · Absorption
Movement of a drug into circulation. Route, blood flow, dose, location and chemical properties influence absorption.
D · Distribution
Movement from circulation into tissues. Lipid solubility, protein binding and tissue perfusion affect distribution.
M · Metabolism
Chemical alteration of a drug, commonly involving the liver. Metabolites may be active or inactive.
E · Excretion
Removal of drug/metabolites, commonly through the kidneys. Liver/kidney function and age can affect elimination.

Half-life is the time required for the amount/concentration of a drug to decrease by about half. It helps explain why medication effects and dosing intervals differ.

Pharmacodynamics: what the drug does to the body

Pharmacodynamics concerns the physiological effects of a drug and its interactions with receptors or other cellular processes. An agonist activates a receptor/process; an antagonist blocks or reduces receptor activity.

Therapeutic effect
The intended beneficial action, such as reducing pain or lowering blood pressure.
Side effect
A predictable unintended effect, such as drowsiness or GI upset.
Adverse reaction
A harmful or clinically significant unintended response.
Interaction
One substance or intervention can alter another's effect. Massage therapists stay within scope and don't attempt medication management.

Medication intake

Ask clients about prescription medications, OTC medications, supplements and relevant topical products. Ask what they take, why they take it, how/where it's administered, and whether they're experiencing side effects. Medication lists can reveal health information a client forgot to mention, but never diagnose a condition solely from a medication.

Don't tell a client to stop, skip, increase, decrease or reschedule a medication for massage. Medication decisions belong to the client and qualified prescriber/pharmacist.

Administration sites and devices

Recent injections
Avoid vigorous local work over a painful, irritated or recently injected site. Follow the client's post-injection instructions rather than relying on one universal waiting period.
Infusion pumps/ports/lines
Avoid pressure, traction, lubricant and heat over medication-delivery hardware. Position for comfort without compromising the device.
Transdermal patches
Don't massage directly over a medication patch. Avoid dislodging it or increasing accidental contact/absorption.
Topical medication
Avoid secondary exposure. Don't spread medicated creams/gels to other body regions with massage lubricant.

Pain and inflammation medications

ClassExamplesMassage implications
NSAIDsIbuprofen, naproxen, celecoxibCan reduce pain/inflammation and may contribute to GI effects or bruising. Pain relief can mask the client's normal protective feedback, so don't use reduced pain as permission for excessive pressure.
CorticosteroidsPrednisone, hydrocortisone, dexamethasoneLong-term/systemic use may be associated with skin fragility, bruising, infection risk and bone loss. Modify according to tissue quality and health status.
Skeletal muscle relaxantsCyclobenzaprine and othersDrowsiness, dizziness and altered sensation may affect pressure feedback and safe transfers.
Opioid analgesicsOxycodone, hydrocodone, fentanyl and othersMay cause sedation, dizziness, constipation and respiratory depression and can significantly mask pain. An impaired or overly sedated client may not be able to participate safely in massage.

Diabetes medications

Insulin and other glucose-lowering medications can contribute to hypoglycemia. Relevant symptoms may include shakiness, sweating, hunger, weakness, confusion, irritability or altered consciousness. Follow facility emergency procedures and the client's established diabetes plan rather than improvising medical treatment outside training.

Also consider injection sites and diabetes-related complications such as peripheral neuropathy, skin integrity and circulation concerns. Reduced sensation means pressure and temperature feedback may be unreliable.

Cardiovascular medications

Anticoagulants / antiplatelets
Examples include warfarin, heparin-type drugs, aspirin and clopidogrel. Increased bruising/bleeding risk may require gentler pressure and avoidance of aggressive techniques.
Vasodilators
May contribute to headache or dizziness. Position changes should be gradual when a client is prone to hypotension.
Beta blockers
Lower heart rate/blood pressure and can cause fatigue or dizziness. Don't assume a client's pulse response will behave like someone not taking these drugs.
ACE inhibitors / ARBs
Used for hypertension and other cardiovascular/renal indications. Dizziness or hypotension can matter for table transfers.
Calcium-channel blockers
Used for hypertension, angina and some rhythm disorders. Dizziness, headache or edema may be relevant.
Diuretics
Increase urine output and may contribute to dizziness or fluid/electrolyte issues. Allow bathroom access and slow transitions as needed.
Statins
Used to reduce lipid-related cardiovascular risk. If a client reports new significant muscle pain or weakness, don't assume massage is the solution; encourage appropriate medical follow-up.
Antiarrhythmics
Medication use signals a cardiac history. Ask about stability and current symptoms; acute cardiac symptoms override the massage plan.

Respiratory and allergy medications

Antihistamines
Some cause significant drowsiness, dizziness or dry mouth. Sedation affects safety and feedback.
Bronchodilators
Used for asthma/COPD and may cause tremor, anxiety, headache or increased heart rate. The bigger question is whether the respiratory condition is stable today.
Antitussives
Cough suppressants may be sedating, especially opioid-containing products. A client with an active contagious respiratory illness should be screened based on illness, not merely medication use.
Decongestants
Some can be stimulating and affect heart rate/blood pressure. Consider the underlying illness and current symptoms.

Neurological and psychiatric medications

Antidepressants, anxiolytics, anticonvulsants, sleep medications and other CNS-active drugs can produce effects such as sedation, dizziness, altered coordination, orthostatic symptoms or changes in sensation. Don't make assumptions about a client's mental-health diagnosis from a medication. Focus on observable effects that influence massage safety.

Antibiotics, antivirals and immune-modifying drugs

The fact that a client takes an antimicrobial isn't automatically the contraindication. Ask why. If they're taking an antibiotic for an active contagious/systemic infection and are febrile or acutely ill, massage may need to be postponed. Long-term immune-suppressing drugs may increase infection vulnerability and can affect skin/tissue integrity depending on the medication.

MBLEx medication strategy

You usually don't need the exact mechanism of every drug. Identify the drug class or relevant side effect, then connect it to the safest massage decision. Anticoagulant → bruising/bleeding caution. Sedative → alertness/transfer concern. Analgesic → masked pain. Antihypertensive → dizziness/position changes. Neuropathy-producing condition or medication → unreliable sensation. Topical/transdermal drug → avoid the application site.

Practice scenarios

  1. Client on warfarin has extensive unexplained bruising: avoid aggressive massage and recognize that unexplained/excessive bleeding findings may warrant medical follow-up.
  2. Client took an opioid and is unusually difficult to keep awake: don't proceed with routine massage just because they booked an appointment. Significant impairment changes consent and safety.
  3. Client has a medicated patch on the upper back: don't massage over it or contaminate your hands/lubricant with medication.
  4. Client using a muscle relaxant becomes dizzy when sitting up: slow the transition, keep them safe and don't rush them off the table.
  5. Client says NSAIDs eliminated pain from yesterday's acute injury and asks for very deep work: medication may be masking protective pain feedback. Screen the injury and choose a conservative approach.
  6. Client on insulin becomes sweaty, shaky and confused: recognize possible hypoglycemia and follow appropriate emergency/client diabetes procedures rather than continuing massage.

What you don't do

Massage therapists don't prescribe, change doses, tell clients to discontinue medication, diagnose adverse reactions, interpret lab values as a prescriber, or claim massage will remove a drug from the body faster. When medication questions exceed massage scope, refer the client to their pharmacist or prescribing healthcare professional.

Mastery check

For Theory Exam #4, students should be able to explain ADME, pharmacodynamics, therapeutic vs side/adverse effects, routes of administration, medication intake, and the massage implications of major drug categories. More importantly, given a medication-related scenario, they should be able to choose the safest modification or referral response.
