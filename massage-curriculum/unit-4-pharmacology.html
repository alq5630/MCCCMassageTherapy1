Pharmacology & Massage Therapy

You don't prescribe medications, but medication use can change sensation, bruising risk, balance, alertness, blood pressure, positioning, and how you plan a massage.

Core language

Pharmacology
Study of drugs, their actions, uses, doses, and effects.
Pharmacokinetics
What the body does to a drug: absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion.
Pharmacodynamics
What the drug does to the body, including receptor interactions and physiological effects.
Therapeutic vs side effect
The intended beneficial action compared with predictable unintended effects.

Medication intake questions

Ask what the client takes, why they take it, how they take it, and whether they experience relevant side effects. Include over-the-counter drugs and supplements. The goal isn't to judge the medication or tell the client to stop it. It's to understand how it affects today's massage.

Administration sites matter

Avoid directly massaging medication patches, infusion devices, pellets, and recent injection sites. Consider the product, route, timing, tissue condition, client instructions, and medical guidance. Don't allow lubricant to contaminate medication-delivery devices.

Common massage-relevant effects

Reduced sensation
Pressure feedback may be less reliable. Use conservative pressure and visual/tissue feedback.
Dizziness or hypotension
Use slower position changes and allow time before standing.
Drowsiness
Monitor alertness and safe transfer off the table.
Bruising or bleeding
Anticoagulant/antiplatelet use may require pressure modification.
Hypoglycemia risk
Diabetes medications can matter when symptoms, meal timing, or activity alter blood glucose.
Skin changes
Some medications can affect skin fragility, healing, or sensitivity.

Medication categories to recognize

Students should be familiar with broad groups including NSAIDs, corticosteroids, muscle relaxants, opioids, diabetes medications, anticoagulants and antiplatelets, antihypertensives, diuretics, statins, antihistamines, bronchodilators, and other common respiratory medications. You don't need to practice pharmacy. You do need to connect a relevant effect to a safe massage modification.
