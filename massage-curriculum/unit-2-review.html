Theory Exam #2 Review

Pull together the theory behind massage effects, evidence, intake, scope, treatment planning, SOAP documentation, reflexology, and the practical routine.

What you should be able to explain

Evidence
Separate a plausible mechanism, a traditional theory, a research finding, and a claim that goes beyond the evidence.
Scope of practice
Know what a massage therapist assesses for treatment planning without diagnosing medical conditions.
Intake & consent
Use health history, goals, contraindication screening, communication, and informed consent to decide whether and how to proceed.
Treatment planning
Connect client goals and findings to positioning, areas treated, techniques, pressure, duration, and modifications.
SOAP documentation
Distinguish Subjective, Objective, Assessment, and Plan information.
Reflexology
Know zones, landmarks, walking, point work, relaxation techniques, treatment guidelines, and contraindications while describing traditional theory responsibly.

SOAP rapid review

S: “My right shoulder has felt tight for three days.”

Subjective. It's information reported by the client.

O: “Right shoulder elevation observed compared with left.”

Objective. It's an observable finding documented by the therapist.

A: “Client tolerated moderate pressure; tissue tension decreased after treatment.”

Assessment. It summarizes the therapist's massage-relevant interpretation and response to treatment without making a medical diagnosis.

P: “Next session, reassess shoulder comfort and continue treatment if appropriate.”

Plan. It describes what happens next.

Reflexology rapid review

How many zones are in the traditional model?

Ten, with five associated with each foot.

What's thumb walking used for?

To work progressively across a treatment area or along a traditional zone while maintaining controlled contact.

Does tenderness at a reflex point diagnose disease in the corresponding organ?

No. A massage therapist shouldn't use a tender foot point as a medical diagnostic test.

Clinical reasoning challenge

A client asks you to “break up toxins” with very deep pressure. What do you do?

Don't reinforce an unsupported explanation. Clarify the client's actual goal, such as soreness or relaxation, explain the treatment in defensible terms, and choose pressure based on comfort and clinical reasoning rather than the toxin claim.

A client reports a new painful, hot, swollen calf. Is this a routine modification?

No. Don't massage the area. A new hot, painful, swollen calf can require medical evaluation and shouldn't be treated as ordinary muscle tension.

A client wants chest work but seems unsure when you explain the draping. What matters most?

Don't proceed until consent is clear and voluntary. The client can decline or change their mind at any time.

What makes a treatment plan individualized?

It responds to that client's goals, history, current presentation, contraindications, preferences, positioning needs, and response to treatment rather than applying the same routine automatically.

Before the exam

Don't just memorize definitions. Practice explaining why you'd choose, modify, or avoid a technique. The strongest exam preparation is being able to move from a client scenario to a safe massage-therapy decision and explain the reasoning behind it.
