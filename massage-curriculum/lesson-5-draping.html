Draping

Draping isn't just covering a body. It's a hands-on professional skill that protects privacy, warmth, boundaries, hygiene and the client's sense of safety while giving the therapist appropriate access to the area being treated.

What is draping?

Draping is the use of linens or similar fabrics to cover the client's body, treatment table and table accessories. Linens are washable fabric goods such as sheets, pillowcases and towels.

Protective barrier
Linens create a barrier between the client's skin and treatment equipment.
Modesty & emotional privacy
Coverage protects personal boundaries and helps the client feel secure.
Warmth
Only the area being treated needs to be exposed, allowing the rest of the body to remain covered.
Professionalism
Controlled, predictable draping communicates competence and appropriate therapeutic boundaries.

The non-negotiable principles

Expose only the area you're actively treating. The drape should remain secure, predictable and appropriate throughout the session.

Tell the client what you're doing when repositioning or changing a drape. Anchor the linen before moving it. Don't lift, wave or fluff the sheet into the air. Keep linens controlled close to the client's body, and never use draping as an excuse for unnecessary exposure.

Consent still matters after the intake. Consent to massage isn't blanket consent to every body region. Sensitive or potentially vulnerable areas require clear communication, appropriate scope, lawful practice and secure draping. If a client doesn't want an area worked, it stays covered.

Clean linen standards

Every client receives clean linens. Linens that contact a client are laundered before reuse. They should be clean, dry and appropriately stored so clean and used laundry don't contaminate one another.

A fresh smell isn't a substitute for actual laundering. Likewise, spraying or wiping a used sheet doesn't make it appropriate for the next client.

Sheet draping

Sheets are the most common draping fabric in massage practice. A basic setup uses at least two sheets: one covering the table and one covering the client. A blanket may be added for warmth without replacing the clean client sheet.

Bottom sheet
Creates the linen barrier between the client and the table.
Top sheet
Covers the client and is manipulated to expose only the treatment region.
Prepare the table
The source chapter recommends folding back the upper corners of the client sheet so the bottom sheet is visible and the client can clearly identify where to get onto the table.
Anchor before moving
Control the edge nearest the area you're exposing so the drape doesn't slide farther than intended.

Draping individual regions

Back
Expose the posterior trunk while maintaining secure coverage of the pelvis and gluteal region according to school policy, law, consent and the technique being performed.
Leg
Expose one lower extremity at a time while maintaining coverage of the opposite leg and pelvic region. Secure the drape before beginning massage.
Arm
Expose the arm and shoulder only as much as needed for the planned work. Re-cover before moving to another region.
Abdomen
Maintain secure chest and pelvic coverage while exposing only the abdomen. A bath towel, folded towel or large pillowcase can provide an additional chest drape when appropriate.
Clinical habit: uncover → treat → re-cover. Don't leave a finished body region exposed while you move elsewhere.

Abdominal draping

Abdominal massage requires particularly deliberate boundaries because the treatment area sits between the chest and pelvis. Establish consent before exposing the abdomen. Keep the breast/chest region securely covered and maintain pelvic coverage.

The Chapter 7 method uses a bath-size towel, folded towel or king-size pillowcase as an additional chest covering. When re-covering the abdomen, anchor the upper drape while drawing the lower covering back into place so coverage remains controlled.

Don't assume abdominal work is routine. Confirm that the technique is within the therapist's scope and training and that the client wants the area treated.

Turning the client

Turning from prone to supine or supine to prone is one of the moments when poor draping technique can create unnecessary exposure. The therapist should guide the process rather than improvising after the client begins moving.

1. Re-cover first
Make sure all previously treated regions are fully covered.
2. Remove obstacles
Remove bolsters or other supports that could interfere with the turn.
3. Adjust position
If the client is prone in a face cradle, have them move appropriately on the table so they can turn safely without the face rest interfering.
4. Anchor the drape
Secure the edge of the sheet or towel nearest you before asking the client to roll.
5. Give one clear instruction
Tell the client which direction to turn and when. Avoid grabbing or physically rolling the client unless there's a specific need and consent.
6. Re-bolster
Once the client is settled, replace the supports appropriate to the new position.
Key concept: the drape stays controlled while the client moves underneath it.

Side-lying draping

Side-lying requires extra attention because the client can feel less stable and the back, hip and lateral body may be treated from the same position. Secure the top sheet before exposing the back or another region.

The Chapter 7 method describes using a towel folded lengthwise to help secure the sheet when the back is exposed. Whatever method is used, the goal is the same: maintain stable coverage of the chest, pelvis and other areas that aren't being treated while allowing enough access for competent bodywork.

Towel draping

Terrycloth towels are thicker, heavier and more absorbent than sheets, which makes them especially useful in hydrotherapy and spa settings. If towels are used to drape the client, the treatment table still needs an appropriate clean linen barrier.

The source chapter teaches a minimum two-towel client setup, with one towel covering the torso and another covering the pelvis and lower extremities. In actual practice, use enough clean coverage to maintain warmth, modesty and security for the client's body size and the service being performed.

Advantages
Weight can make a towel feel secure, and terrycloth absorbs moisture well.
Limitations
A towel covers less area than a sheet, so the therapist must be especially deliberate during movement and repositioning.

Towel draping during a turn

Before the client turns, remove bolsters, fully cover the client and organize the towels so they remain secure. The Chapter 7 sequence includes rotating the lower towel so the torso and lower-body towels are oriented parallel to one another, then anchoring the towel edges nearest the therapist while the client rolls.

The exact hand sequence can vary by school and workplace, but the outcome can't: private areas remain covered throughout the turn.

Professional boundaries & draping

Draping is part of the therapeutic boundary, not merely a technique for keeping a client warm. A therapist shouldn't expose more tissue because it's convenient, because a client says they "don't care," or because a particular therapist has become casual about draping over time.

Explain
Tell the client how they should undress, where they'll be covered and how you'll leave the room before the session begins.
Ask
Get permission before working areas that weren't clearly established in the treatment plan.
Observe
If the client becomes tense, grabs the sheet, hesitates or verbally expresses discomfort, stop and address it.
Respect
The client's choice to remain more clothed or more covered doesn't need justification.

Draping lab

Set the table with a clean bottom sheet, top sheet and optional blanket.
Practice a back drape without lifting or fluffing the sheet.
Practice right and left leg draping while keeping the pelvis and opposite leg securely covered.
Practice arm and shoulder draping in both prone and supine.
Practice abdominal draping using a separate chest towel or pillowcase.
Guide your partner through prone-to-supine and supine-to-prone turns without loss of coverage.
Practice securing a side-lying back drape.
Repeat the turn using a two-towel draping setup.
Have your partner deliberately say "I don't want my abdomen uncovered" and practice responding professionally without questioning the preference.

MBLEx-style check

What is the primary purpose of draping?

There isn't only one purpose. Draping protects modesty and emotional privacy, provides warmth and security, creates a protective linen barrier and supports professional boundaries.

What should happen to linens after they've been used with a client?

They should be placed with used laundry and laundered before reuse.

Why should a therapist avoid fluffing or lifting the top sheet?

It reduces control of the drape and can create unnecessary exposure or insecurity.

Before a prone client turns supine, what's an important safety step?

Re-cover the client and remove bolsters or other obstacles that could interfere with the turn.

A client tells you they don't want their abdomen uncovered. What's the best response?

Respect the request and leave the abdomen covered. The treatment plan can be modified without requiring the client to defend the boundary.

What should be exposed during massage?

Only the area currently being treated, with appropriate secure coverage of the rest of the body.

Why might towels be useful in hydrotherapy?

Terrycloth is thicker and highly absorbent, making towels useful when moisture is involved.

Does a client's statement that they "don't care about draping" eliminate the therapist's professional responsibility?

No. The therapist still maintains professional, lawful and appropriate draping and boundaries.
