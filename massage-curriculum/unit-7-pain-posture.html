Pain & Posture Assessment

Assessment gives you information for massage planning. It doesn't turn observation into diagnosis.

Pain is multidimensional

Pain isn't a direct meter of tissue damage. Biological input, attention, expectation, emotion, previous experience, context and nervous-system processing all influence pain. A client's pain report is real information even when the amount of pain doesn't match a simple tissue-damage model.

Useful pain questions

Location
Where is it? Local or spreading?
Quality
Aching, sharp, burning, throbbing, electrical?
Behavior
What makes it better or worse?
Timing
When did it begin? Constant or intermittent?
Intensity
Use a consistent scale as one data point, not the whole assessment.
Function
What can the client no longer do comfortably?

Postural observation

Observe from anterior, lateral and posterior views. Note relative head position, shoulder height, spinal curves, pelvic orientation, knee position and foot/ankle alignment. Describe what you see without assigning a pathology from posture alone.

Assessment sequence

History → observation → active movement → passive movement when appropriate → palpation → treatment → reassessment. Not every client needs every step. Choose tests that answer a treatment-planning question.

Red flags

Unexpected neurological loss, severe unexplained pain, major trauma, suspected fracture or clot, systemic illness signs, bowel/bladder changes with neurological symptoms, or other findings outside massage scope require referral rather than increasingly aggressive assessment.
