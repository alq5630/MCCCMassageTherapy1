Pain & Posture Assessment

Assessment gives you information for massage planning. It doesn't turn observation into diagnosis. The goal is to gather useful information, identify safety concerns, form a treatment hypothesis, and reassess what changes.

Pain is multidimensional

Pain isn't a direct meter of tissue damage. Biological input, attention, expectation, emotion, previous experience, context and nervous-system processing all influence pain. A client's pain report is real information even when the amount of pain doesn't match a simple tissue-damage model.

Instead of asking only “where does it hurt?”, ask what the pain means for the client's life. Function often gives you more useful treatment information than a number alone.

Build the pain picture

Location
Where is it? Local or spreading? Superficial or deep?
Quality
Aching, sharp, burning, throbbing, electrical, pulling, heavy?
Behavior
What movements, positions or activities make it better or worse?
Timing
When did it begin? Constant or intermittent? Changing over time?
Intensity
Use a consistent scale as one data point, not the entire assessment.
Function
What activity, sleep position, work task or movement is limited?

Postural observation

Observe from anterior, lateral and posterior views. Note relative head position, shoulder height, spinal curves, pelvic orientation, knee position and foot/ankle alignment. Describe what you see without assigning a pathology from posture alone.

Useful language sounds like “right shoulder appears higher in standing” rather than “your upper trapezius is causing your headaches.” Observation creates questions. It doesn't prove causation.

Assessment sequence

  1. History: clarify the client's concern, goals, relevant health history and symptom behavior.
  2. Observation: notice posture, guarding, swelling, skin changes and ease of movement.
  3. Active movement: the client moves themselves. Note range, symptoms, confidence and quality.
  4. Passive movement: when appropriate, compare what changes when the therapist moves the region.
  5. Palpation: assess tissue qualities relevant to your treatment question.
  6. Treatment: choose techniques that fit the information you've gathered.
  7. Reassessment: repeat something meaningful to see whether the treatment changed pain, range, comfort or function.

Not every client needs every step. Choose assessments because they answer a question, not because you want to perform every test you know.

Active vs. passive movement

If active movement is uncomfortable but passive movement is substantially easier, that difference is useful information. If both are restricted or painful, that's also useful. Massage therapists can use these patterns to guide treatment and referral decisions, but shouldn't turn them into a medical diagnosis.

Palpation without overclaiming

Palpation can identify tenderness, temperature differences, tissue texture, tone, swelling, movement and the client's response to pressure. Words like “tight,” “ropey,” “dense” or “tender” describe what you're perceiving. They don't establish a disease process by themselves.

Red flags change the plan

Unexpected neurological loss, severe unexplained pain, major trauma, suspected fracture or clot, systemic illness signs, bowel/bladder changes with neurological symptoms, rapidly worsening symptoms, or other findings outside massage scope require referral rather than increasingly aggressive assessment.

Clinical rule: when your concern becomes “what serious condition could this be?”, the massage table isn't where you prove the diagnosis.

Partner assessment lab

  1. Have your partner choose a non-acute area of ordinary stiffness or discomfort.
  2. Conduct a brief intake and identify one functional goal.
  3. Observe posture without telling a story about what it “means.”
  4. Choose one relevant active movement and, if appropriate, passive movement.
  5. Palpate only what helps answer your treatment question.
  6. State a massage treatment hypothesis in scope: “I want to see whether working X changes Y.”
  7. Perform a short treatment.
  8. Repeat the original movement or functional measure.
  9. Document what changed and what didn't.

Clinical reasoning check

A client has rounded shoulders. Does that prove the pectorals are causing their neck pain?

No. Posture is an observation, not proof of causation. Use history, movement, palpation, response to treatment and the client's goals to guide your plan.

Why reassess after treatment?

Reassessment tells you whether the intervention changed something meaningful. It helps move massage from “I did techniques” toward “I tested whether my treatment helped the goal.”

What should you do if an assessment produces new neurological symptoms?

Stop. New neurological findings can require referral. Don't keep adding pressure or tests in an attempt to solve the problem yourself.
