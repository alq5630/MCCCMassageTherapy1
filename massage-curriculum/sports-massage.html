Sports Massage

Sports massage is goal-driven massage adapted to the athlete, activity, timing and tissue state. The same athlete may need a very different session before competition than after it.

Start with the goal

Don't choose techniques simply because the client is an athlete. Ask what they're doing, when they're doing it, what tissues are stressed by the activity, whether there's an injury history, and what they need from today's session. Sports massage can support readiness, comfort, recovery, mobility and body awareness, but it doesn't guarantee injury prevention or enhanced performance.

Four common contexts

Pre-event
Short, stimulating and specific. The goal is readiness, not deep relaxation or creating soreness.
Inter-event
Brief work between heats, rounds or events. Maintain comfort and readiness without fatiguing the athlete.
Post-event
Calmer recovery-oriented work after activity. Screen carefully for acute injury before treating painful areas.
Maintenance
Sessions away from competition can be longer and more comprehensive, addressing recurring tension, mobility and training demands.

Pre-event massage

Keep it brisk and relatively superficial. Common choices include rhythmic effleurage, compression, petrissage, vibration and light tapotement. Focus on the muscles most relevant to the upcoming activity. Avoid unfamiliar aggressive work, prolonged deep pressure or anything likely to leave the athlete sore.

Inter-event massage

Time is limited, so reassess what the athlete needs now. Gentle circulation-oriented strokes, comfortable ROM, brief stretching, compression and breathing cues may be appropriate. The goal is to help the athlete feel ready to return to activity, not to perform a full corrective treatment.

Post-event massage

Transition toward slower, comfortable work. Effleurage, gentle petrissage, compression and comfortable passive movement may help the athlete relax and perceive less soreness or stiffness. If there is focal pain, swelling, bruising, loss of function or suspected acute injury, don't treat it as ordinary post-exercise tightness.

A science correction students should know

Massage doesn't need a “lactic acid flushing” explanation to be useful. Lactate produced during exercise is cleared and reused rapidly through normal metabolism. Sports massage is better explained through effects such as sensory input, relaxation, perceived soreness, comfort, mobility and short-term changes in tissue tolerance rather than claiming that massage mechanically removes metabolic waste.

Maintenance and return after injury

Maintenance massage can incorporate Swedish work, focused deep-tissue techniques, MFR, trigger point therapy, friction when appropriate, ROM and stretching. A history of injury or surgery changes assessment and technique selection. Massage therapists don't diagnose an injury or direct rehabilitation outside their scope. Coordinate with the athlete's healthcare or rehabilitation plan when one exists.

Technique toolbox

Swedish strokes
Effleurage, petrissage, compression, vibration and tapotement can be adjusted for timing and desired nervous-system effect.
Mobility work
PROM, active movement, static stretching and RI/PIR can be integrated when appropriate.
Focused tissue work
MFR and trigger point therapy remain distinct techniques and are selected based on findings, not automatically because the athlete reports tightness.
Friction
Use thoughtfully for a specific tissue goal and never as an automatic attempt to “break up scar tissue.”

Screening

Sports participation doesn't cancel ordinary massage contraindications. Screen for fever/systemic illness, skin infection, open wounds, suspected DVT, acute fracture or significant sprain/strain, severe unexplained swelling, altered sensation and other red flags. Recent surgery or active rehabilitation may require modification and coordination with the client's care plan.

Build a treatment plan

For any athlete, students should be able to answer: What sport or activity? What phase of training or competition? What is today's goal? What muscles and movements are most involved? What findings change the plan? Which techniques fit the timing? What should be avoided? How will you reassess?

Documentation

Document the athlete's stated goal, relevant history, observations, techniques, areas treated, client response and plan. SOAP format can organize this information, but good notes should remain objective and within massage scope.
