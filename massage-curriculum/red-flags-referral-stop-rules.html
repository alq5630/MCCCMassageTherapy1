Red Flags, Stop Rules & Referral

Massage therapists do not need to diagnose the emergency to recognize that massage is no longer the appropriate next step.

Emergency patterns

Possible stroke: sudden facial weakness, arm weakness, speech difficulty or other sudden focal neurological change.

Possible cardiac emergency: concerning chest pressure/pain, severe shortness of breath, sweating, collapse or associated symptoms.

Possible pulmonary embolism: sudden unexplained shortness of breath, chest pain, rapid deterioration, especially with DVT risk/signs.

Anaphylaxis: rapid airway/tongue/throat swelling, breathing difficulty or systemic collapse.

Unresponsiveness/severe breathing problem: activate emergency response and provide care within training.

Urgent referral / do not massage the problem away

New unilateral hot swollen tender calf; progressive neurological weakness; major acute trauma or suspected fracture; spreading painful skin infection; unexplained severe symptoms; significant new swelling; new concerning mass/lump; persistent unexplained symptoms outside massage scope.

Neurological stop rules

Electrical, shooting or radiating symptoms created/worsened by your technique; sudden numbness; new weakness; loss of coordination; severe dizziness; altered mental status. Stop the provoking technique, reassess and refer/escalate according to severity.

Communication

Use neutral language: “This is outside what I can safely assess or treat with massage, and I think it should be medically evaluated.” Avoid frightening diagnostic statements such as “You definitely have a blood clot” unless that diagnosis has actually been made by an appropriate provider.

Documentation

Record what the client reported, what you observed, when it occurred, what action you took and any referral/emergency response. Document facts rather than retrospective diagnosis.
