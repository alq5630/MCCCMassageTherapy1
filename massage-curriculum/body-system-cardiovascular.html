Cardiovascular System

Follow one red blood cell through the circuit and most cardiovascular anatomy becomes easier to remember.

Blood flow through the heart

Body → venae cavae → right atrium → tricuspid valve → right ventricle → pulmonary valve → pulmonary arteries → lungs → pulmonary veins → left atrium → mitral/bicuspid valve → left ventricle → aortic valve → aorta → body.

Important exception to the usual oxygen rule: pulmonary arteries carry deoxygenated blood and pulmonary veins carry oxygenated blood.

Heart structure

The heart has four chambers and four major valves. The right side pumps pulmonary circulation; the left side pumps systemic circulation. The left ventricular wall is thicker because it must generate pressure for systemic circulation.

Electrical conduction

The SA node normally initiates the heartbeat, followed by conduction through atrial tissue to the AV node, AV bundle/bundle branches and Purkinje system to coordinate ventricular contraction.

Cardiac cycle

Systole refers to contraction/ejection phases, especially ventricular systole in blood-pressure language. Diastole refers to relaxation/filling. Heart sounds primarily reflect valve closure.

Blood vessels

ArteriesCarry blood away from heart under relatively higher pressure; walls contain substantial smooth muscle/elastic tissue.
ArteriolesMajor resistance vessels regulating flow into capillary beds.
CapillariesMicroscopic exchange vessels with thin walls.
VenulesCollect blood from capillary beds.
VeinsReturn blood to heart; many limb veins contain valves that help limit backflow.

Blood

Plasma is the liquid matrix. Erythrocytes/RBCs transport respiratory gases via hemoglobin. Leukocytes/WBCs participate in immune defense. Platelets are cell fragments important in hemostasis/clotting.

Blood pressure

Blood pressure reflects forces within the arterial system and is influenced by cardiac output, vascular resistance, blood volume and other regulatory factors. Systolic is the higher pressure associated with ventricular ejection; diastolic is the lower pressure during ventricular relaxation.

A massage therapist does not diagnose hypertension from one casual reading or use an outdated single number as a universal massage cutoff. Symptoms, severity, stability, medical history and current guidance matter.

Venous return

Skeletal muscle contraction, breathing-related pressure changes and venous valves assist return from the limbs. Avoid claiming massage is required to “push blood back to the heart.” The cardiovascular system already has powerful intrinsic pumps and pressure gradients.

DVT / embolic warning

Possible DVT can include unilateral swelling, warmth, pain/tenderness and color change. Do not massage a suspected DVT. If a clot embolizes to the lungs, symptoms may include sudden shortness of breath, chest pain, rapid heart rate, fainting or coughing blood and require emergency care.

Conditions & massage reasoning

Hypertension: stable, medically managed hypertension often calls for appropriate monitoring/modification rather than a blanket ban. Varicose veins: avoid aggressive direct pressure over compromised superficial veins. Raynaud phenomenon: episodic vasospasm causes color/sensation changes, often in fingers/toes; avoid extreme temperature exposure and adapt to symptoms. Heart disease: type, severity, symptoms, medication and medical stability determine planning.

Endangerment-site connections

Major vascular caution regions include the carotid sinus/arteries in the neck, subclavian/axillary vessels, antecubital fossa, abdominal aorta, femoral triangle, popliteal fossa and superficial varicose veins. A pulse under your finger is anatomical information, not a target for sustained deep pressure.
