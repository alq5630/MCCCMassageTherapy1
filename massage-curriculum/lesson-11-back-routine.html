Beginning the Back Routine

Build a safe prone sequence while connecting palpation, kinesiology, technique selection, client feedback, contraindications, and therapist body mechanics.

Set up first

Client
Prone with face support adjusted and ankles or other regions supported according to comfort.
Therapist
Use a stable stance, soft knees, neutral wrists, and efficient body-weight transfer.
Clinical goal
Establish contact, assess tissue, warm the region, perform appropriate focused work, reassess, and integrate.

Know what's under your hands

Erector spinae
Long paraspinal muscle group involved in spinal extension and ipsilateral lateral flexion. Palpate lateral to the spinous processes rather than pressing directly on bone.
Trapezius
Upper fibers elevate the scapula, middle fibers retract it, and lower fibers contribute to depression. The muscle also participates in scapular upward rotation.
Rhomboid major & minor
Deep to trapezius between the vertebral column and medial scapular border. They retract and downwardly rotate the scapula.
Latissimus dorsi
Large superficial back muscle that extends, adducts, and medially rotates the humerus.
Scapular landmarks
Practice locating the spine, acromion, medial border, inferior angle, and surrounding soft tissue.
Suboccipital region
Small deep muscles near the occiput participate in fine head and upper-cervical movement. Use precise, comfortable contact rather than aggressive pressure.
MBLEx habit: connect every muscle to location → action → palpation → safe treatment choice. Don't memorize isolated facts if you can understand the relationship.

Beginning sequence

Grounding contact and broad compression
Establish predictable contact and assess general tone, temperature, tenderness, and client response.
Broad effleurage
Use smooth gliding strokes to introduce movement and pressure. Let your stance drive the stroke.
Explore the paraspinals
Trace soft tissue lateral to the spinous processes. Avoid inappropriate direct pressure over bony prominences.
Work the scapular region
Use gliding, compression, petrissage, or controlled friction as appropriate while identifying landmarks and respecting endangerment areas.
Introduce broad forearm work
Use a comfortable contact surface and transfer body weight without collapsing the wrist, shoulder, or spine.
Suboccipital and scalp connection
Use controlled contact at the base of the skull and comfortable scalp techniques while monitoring pressure and client response.
Reassess and integrate
Return to broad contact. Compare tissue feel and client feedback with the beginning of the sequence rather than assuming the treatment produced a specific change.

Clinical reasoning during a routine

Before
Review intake, goals, symptoms, medications, contraindications, positioning needs, and consent.
During
Monitor tissue response, guarding, pain, temperature, skin changes, breathing, and verbal feedback.
Modify
Change pressure, technique, positioning, duration, or region when the client's condition or response requires it.
After
Reassess relevant findings, communicate without diagnosing, and document what was done and how the client responded.

Partner practice checkoff

Position and bolster the prone client comfortably.
Identify erector spinae, trapezius, rhomboid region, latissimus dorsi, scapular borders, and occipital ridge.
Name at least one major action of trapezius, rhomboids, latissimus dorsi, and erector spinae.
Demonstrate broad compression, effleurage, petrissage, and an appropriate friction technique.
Use forearm pressure with efficient body mechanics.
Identify any bony landmarks or vulnerable areas where pressure should be modified.
Ask for feedback at clinically useful points rather than continuously interrupting the session.
Reassess the region at the end and describe observations without making a diagnosis.

MBLEx-style check

A client retracts the scapula. Which muscles are strongly associated with that action?

Rhomboids and middle trapezius are major scapular retractors.

Which muscle extends, adducts, and medially rotates the humerus?

Latissimus dorsi.

Why should you avoid pressing directly on the spinous processes?

They're bony landmarks rather than the soft tissue target, and direct pressure may be uncomfortable or inappropriate.

A client begins guarding during deeper work. What's the best next step?

Reduce or stop the provoking technique, check in, and modify based on the client's response rather than pushing through guarding.

Why reassess after focused work?

Reassessment helps determine the client's response and whether the treatment plan should continue, change, or stop.
