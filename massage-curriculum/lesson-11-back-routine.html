Beginning the Back Routine

Now the foundations become a massage. This lesson starts the prone back and scalp sequence using your routine workbook's positioning, body mechanics, palpation cues, and technique integration.

Set up first

Client
Prone with ankles comfortably bolstered and face support adjusted.
Therapist
Begin at the head of the table in a grounded horse stance. Keep knees soft and hinge from the hips.
Focus
Warm-up, tissue assessment, circulation, fascial mobility, relaxation, and connection.

Structures you'll start learning under your hands

Erector spinae: trace the long paraspinal columns lateral to the spinous processes from the sacral region toward the upper back.

Trapezius: palpate from the base of the neck toward the acromion. A slight shoulder shrug can help students feel contraction.

Rhomboids: located between the medial border of the scapula and spine. Scapular retraction helps clarify their location.

Suboccipitals: small deep muscles beneath the occiput. Work here requires gentle, precise contact rather than aggressive pressure.

Student habit to build now: don't just perform strokes. Keep asking yourself what structure is under your hand, what direction its fibers run, how the tissue feels, and whether it changes as you work.

Beginning sequence

Grounding contact and broad compression
Establish calm, confident contact. Use broad palmar compressions over the back to introduce pressure and feel general tissue tone.
Warm with broad effleurage
Use long, smooth gliding strokes that follow the contours of the back. Maintain continuous contact and let your stance move the stroke.
Explore the paraspinals
Use palms or appropriate finger contact to trace the erector spinae without pressing directly onto the spinous processes.
Work the trapezius and scapular region
Use broad gliding, compression, kneading, and controlled circular friction as appropriate while respecting bony landmarks.
Introduce forearm effleurage
Use the forearm for broad pressure while keeping the elbow below the shoulder and transferring force from the legs and core rather than the wrist.
Suboccipital and scalp connection
Use gentle contact at the base of the skull and light fingertip or raking movements through the scalp. Match the pace to the client's relaxation response.
Integrate and close the region
Return to broader, smoother strokes so the detailed work reconnects into a whole-back experience rather than feeling like isolated techniques.

Body mechanics throughout

Keep a wide, stable base when working from the head of the table. Hinge instead of rounding your back. Let body weight create pressure. Keep shoulders relaxed and wrists neutral. When changing positions around the table, transition smoothly and maintain appropriate contact so the client isn't surprised by your movement.

Partner practice checkoff

Position and bolster the prone client comfortably.
Demonstrate a grounded horse stance and hip hinge.
Identify the erector spinae, trapezius, scapular borders, and occipital ridge.
Perform broad palmar compression without abrupt or poking pressure.
Perform smooth effleurage with continuous contact.
Use forearm pressure without collapsing the wrist, shoulder, or spine.
Demonstrate a controlled friction technique in an appropriate area.
Transition from back to scalp work and back to broad integration without breaking the flow.
Ask for pressure and comfort feedback at appropriate points rather than constantly interrupting the session.

Quick self-check

What muscle group runs parallel to the spine?

The erector spinae group.

What stance does the workbook emphasize at the head of the table?

Horse stance.

Where should force for forearm work come from?

From efficient body-weight transfer and the therapist's core and lower body, not muscular strain through the wrist and shoulder.

Why return to broad effleurage after more specific work?

It reconnects the region, helps reassess the tissue, and creates a smoother transition in the massage sequence.
