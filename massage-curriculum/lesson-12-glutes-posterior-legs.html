Glutes & Posterior Legs

Continue directly from the completed back sequence into the instructor's New Routine for glutes, posterior thighs, calves and feet. Learn the choreography, but also know the anatomy under every contact.

Use the New Routine as the source of truth

The sequence and nicknames on this page follow the current 60-minute New Routine. Classroom demonstration controls the exact hand placement, depth and draping. The website adds anatomy, reasoning, safety and checkoff criteria so you're learning a professional skill rather than memorizing a dance.

Regional anatomy map

Gluteus maximus
Large superficial hip extensor. Its fibers cover much of the posterior hip and contribute to the gluteal contour.
Gluteus medius & minimus
Lateral hip abductors and pelvic stabilizers. Minimus lies deep to medius.
Piriformis
Deep posterior hip muscle running from the sacral region toward the greater trochanter. This is a focused region, not a reason to blindly press hard into the buttock.
Hamstrings
Biceps femoris laterally, semitendinosus and semimembranosus medially. Most originate at the ischial tuberosity and cross the posterior thigh.
Adductors
Medial-thigh muscles. Their location makes draping, consent, hand direction and professional boundaries especially important.
IT band
A dense lateral fascial structure rather than a muscle belly. Don't treat it as tissue that must be aggressively “broken up.”
Gastrocnemius
Superficial calf with medial and lateral heads. It crosses the knee and ankle.
Soleus
Deep to gastrocnemius and important in plantar flexion. Calf work should account for both superficial and deeper tissue layers.
Plantar tissues
The sole contains plantar fascia plus intrinsic foot muscles, tendons, nerves and vessels. Use the calcaneus and arches as landmarks.
Popliteal space
Posterior knee is an endangerment area. Don't drive focused pressure into the popliteal fossa.

The New Routine sequence

Glutes over the sheet · about 2 minutes each side
Glute Press
Begin with broad palmar compressions over the drape. Establish the region and pressure before moving into more focused contact.
Rock & Roll
Use forearm rocking into the gluteal and sacral region as demonstrated. Keep the movement broad and rhythmic.
Elbow Melt
Slowly sink the elbow into the appropriate piriformis region. Gradual loading matters. This is not a jab, and deeper isn't automatically better.
Target Points
Use controlled thumb pressure for the gluteus medius, gluteus minimus and piriformis regions as taught. Palpate first, then choose pressure.
Fist Transition
Use broad fist compression while transitioning to the opposite side, then repeat the glute sequence.
Posterior leg · about 4 minutes each side
Leg Sweep
Effleurage from the foot toward the hip. Use the opening pass to orient yourself to tissue, temperature, comfort and landmarks.
Ham Glide
Forearm glide up the hamstrings. Keep the contact in muscular tissue and control depth as you approach proximal attachments.
Adductor Wring
Use the fingertip wringing technique along the appropriate medial-thigh tissue with secure draping and deliberate professional hand placement.
Glute Fold Glide
Use the elbow/forearm glide across the gluteal-fold region exactly as demonstrated, maintaining clear boundaries and avoiding indiscriminate pressure near vulnerable structures.
IT/Thigh Fist Flow
Use a soft fist along the lateral thigh/hamstring region. Think broad tissue engagement rather than trying to crush the IT band.
Calf Effleurage
Transition into the lower leg with gliding contact. Adjust pressure around the Achilles tendon, tibial border and posterior knee.
Calf Split
Use the thumbs to distinguish and work between the medial and lateral gastrocnemius heads. Don't drive superiorly into the popliteal space.
Lobster Claw
Perform the taught cross-fiber petrissage through the calf. The movement should gather and mobilize tolerant soft tissue, not pinch skin.
Heel Hold
Grasp and gently traction the heel. Keep the ankle supported and the movement controlled.
Plantar Path
Use thumb or knuckle work through the appropriate plantar fascia and arch regions. Follow the foot's anatomy instead of applying random pressure to the sole.
Redrape & Repeat
Securely redrape the completed leg, move to the opposite side and repeat the full posterior-leg sequence.

What each technique is actually doing

Glute Press
Broad rhythmic or sustained compression.
Rock & Roll
Broad compression + rocking/oscillation.
Elbow Melt
Focused sustained compression with gradual loading.
Leg Sweep
Long effleurage.
Ham Glide
Deeper forearm effleurage/gliding.
Adductor Wring
A wringing tissue-mobilization action related to petrissage/friction depending on the exact classroom application.
Calf Split
Focused gliding/stripping used to distinguish muscular contours.
Lobster Claw
Cross-fiber petrissage.
Heel Hold
Traction and supported joint contact.
Plantar Path
Focused thumb/knuckle gliding and compression through tolerant plantar tissues.

Posterior-leg safety is non-negotiable

The posterior leg contains regions where “more pressure” can become unsafe very quickly. Avoid deep focused pressure in the popliteal fossa. Be alert to unexplained unilateral calf swelling, warmth, redness, significant tenderness or other signs that could suggest a vascular problem. Don't massage a suspicious calf and “see if it loosens.” Stop and follow appropriate referral/emergency procedures.

Also modify for bruising, acute strains, recent surgery, skin lesions, varicosities, altered sensation, acute inflammation and other local or systemic contraindications covered elsewhere in the curriculum.

Glute and medial-thigh professionalism

These are sensitive regions. Consent isn't implied just because the client consented to “a massage.” Explain what you're doing, maintain secure draping, expose only what is appropriate for the technique, and make it easy for the client to decline or modify work. Follow school policy, state law and professional standards.

Over-sheet glute work is intentionally part of this New Routine. Don't turn the routine into undraped glute work unless that technique has been separately taught, consented to and permitted.

Palpation before pressure

Ischial tuberosity
Know the proximal hamstring landmark so focused work doesn't become guesswork.
Greater trochanter
Key lateral-hip landmark for orienting the gluteal region.
Femur
Track the thigh's broad muscular compartments around the underlying bone.
Fibular head
Important lateral-knee landmark. Avoid aggressive pressure around vulnerable neural structures.
Tibia
The tibial border is bone, not a target for deep soft-tissue pressure.
Calcaneus & Achilles
Use them to orient heel and plantar work and adjust force appropriately.

Practical checkoff

Perform Glute Press → Rock & Roll → Elbow Melt → Target Points → Fist Transition in order.
Identify the greater trochanter, sacrum and general piriformis region before focused glute pressure.
Perform the posterior-leg sequence in the exact New Routine order without instructor prompting.
Name the hamstrings and identify medial versus lateral posterior-thigh tissue.
Demonstrate secure draping and communication before medial-thigh/adductor work.
Locate the popliteal space and explain why focused deep pressure is avoided there.
Differentiate the medial and lateral gastrocnemius heads before Calf Split.
Perform Lobster Claw without pinching or losing rhythm.
Demonstrate supported Heel Hold traction and anatomically directed Plantar Path work.
Redrape efficiently and repeat the sequence on the second side while maintaining flow and body mechanics.

MBLEx + clinical reasoning check

Which major endangerment area lies behind the knee?

The popliteal space/fossa. Important neurovascular structures pass through this region, so deep focused pressure isn't appropriate.

What are the three hamstring muscles?

Biceps femoris, semitendinosus and semimembranosus.

Is the IT band a muscle belly that should be aggressively kneaded?

No. It's a dense fascial structure. Work in the lateral thigh should be anatomically informed and tolerable rather than based on trying to “break up” the band.

A client's calf is newly swollen, warm and unusually tender on one side. What should you do?

Don't massage it. A potentially serious vascular problem requires appropriate medical evaluation rather than massage.

Why is consent especially important for gluteal and medial-thigh work?

They're sensitive regions requiring clear boundaries, secure draping, informed client agreement and adherence to professional and legal standards.

What technique family best describes Glute Press?

Compression.

What does Heel Hold primarily add to the routine?

Supported contact and gentle traction at the foot/ankle before focused plantar work.
