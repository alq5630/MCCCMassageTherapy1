Contraindications Decision Guide

Contraindications are not one giant list. The skill is deciding whether to postpone, avoid a region, modify the technique, or gather more information.

The four-question screen

1. Is the client systemically unwell or medically unstable?
2. Is there a specific local tissue/structure that should not be treated?
3. Can the session be made safe by changing pressure, position, duration, temperature or technique?
4. Is essential information missing?

Decision categories

CategoryMeaningExamples of reasoning
Postpone / systemicMassage is inappropriate until the acute/systemic issue resolves or is medically clarified.Significant contagious illness, acute systemic infection, medical emergency, suspected DVT, severe unexplained acute symptoms.
Local contraindicationMassage elsewhere may be reasonable, but avoid the affected area.Open wound, acute localized infection, unstable/recent fracture region, inflamed lesion, certain devices/ports, acute nerve irritation, localized rash depending on cause.
ModificationMassage can proceed with altered technique.Fragile skin, osteoporosis risk, sensory loss, medications that increase bruising risk, pregnancy positioning, older adult tissue tolerance.
Needs more informationDo not guess. Clarify diagnosis, symptoms, treatment, physician restrictions or current status.Unexplained swelling, new lump, unclear surgery restrictions, changing neurological symptoms, unknown skin eruption.

Five classic signs of acute inflammation/infection

Heat (calor), redness (rubor), swelling (tumor), pain (dolor), and loss/diminishment of function (functio laesa). Pus/drainage can further indicate infection. These signs require clinical context, but an obviously acute infected area is not something to massage through.

Local examples from your course deck

Athlete's foot: avoid affected infectious skin and prevent cross-contamination. Acute nerve pain: avoid rough/direct irritating work and reassess. Fracture: avoid an unhealed/unstable fracture region and follow medical restrictions. Inguinal hernia: do not perform intrusive abdominal/groin pressure over the hernia. Eye/contact lens considerations: avoid eye pressure and adapt positioning. Dentures: avoid facial techniques that create uncomfortable pulling/displacement.

Vascular red flags

Unexplained unilateral limb swelling, warmth, redness/discoloration and pain/tenderness can raise concern for a thrombotic process. Do not massage or heat a suspected DVT. New chest pain, shortness of breath, fainting, coughing blood or other emergency symptoms require emergency medical response.

Medication changes the decision

Anticoagulants/antiplatelet drugs can increase bruising/bleeding risk. Sedating medications may change alertness. Medications or conditions that alter sensation make pressure and thermal feedback less reliable. Long-term corticosteroid use may affect tissue/bone integrity. The correct response is usually individualized modification and history-taking, not declaring every medication an absolute contraindication.

Routine checkpoints

NeckScreen neurological/vascular symptoms. Avoid carotid/anterior airway compression.
Chest/PecConsent, draping, breast-tissue boundaries, ports/devices and surgical history.
AbdomenAsk about pregnancy, surgery, hernia, acute GI symptoms and unexplained pain.
GlutesConsent/boundaries plus radiating neurological symptoms.
LegsScreen swelling, warmth, varicosities, wounds, sensory changes and vascular risk.
FeetInspect for infectious skin/fungal conditions, wounds and sensory loss before contact.

Scenario practice

Client has a healing paper cut on one finger.

Usually a local issue. Avoid contaminating the wound, use appropriate infection-control measures, and massage other safe areas.

Client has feverish systemic illness and feels weak.

Postpone. The concern is the client's systemic condition and possible transmission, not merely one body region.

Client takes an anticoagulant and bruises easily.

Medication history supports pressure modification and avoiding aggressive techniques likely to cause bruising. It is not automatically a blanket ban on all massage.

Client has numb feet from neuropathy.

Modify pressure and thermal applications because sensory feedback may be unreliable. Inspect tissue and avoid depending on “tell me if it hurts” as the only safety check.

Client reports new calf swelling and warmth after a long flight.

Do not massage the leg. Possible DVT needs prompt medical evaluation.

Remember

The safest therapist is not the one who memorizes the longest “never massage” list. It is the therapist who recognizes risk, knows anatomy, asks the right questions, stays within scope, modifies intelligently and refers when the situation exceeds massage therapy.
