Treatment Planning, Informed Consent & Documentation

A good massage isn't just a routine performed correctly. The therapist gathers information, agrees on goals, chooses an appropriate plan, obtains ongoing consent, adapts the session and records what matters.

From intake to treatment

1 · Assess
Gather relevant health history, client goals, symptoms, preferences, observations, contraindications and functional information within scope.
2 · Prioritize
Decide what matters most today. A client may have several complaints, but session time, tolerance and safety create priorities.
3 · Plan
Choose regions, positioning, techniques, pressure, approximate time allocation and modifications that fit the client's goals.
4 · Consent
Explain the proposed work clearly enough that the client can agree, decline or request changes.
5 · Treat + reassess
Client response is new assessment information. Pressure, positioning and techniques can change during the session.
6 · Document
Record relevant information, work performed, modifications, response and future plan in a professional record.

Treatment goals vs. technique names

Students often plan by listing techniques: “effleurage, petrissage, friction.” Clinical planning starts one level higher. Ask what are we trying to accomplish, and why is this technique appropriate for this client today?

Goal: general relaxation
Plan broad rhythmic contact, comfortable pressure, smooth transitions and enough time for the nervous system to settle.
Goal: focused muscular tension
Use assessment to identify relevant tissue and select focused techniques without assuming every tender spot needs maximum pressure.
Goal: improve comfortable movement
Consider soft-tissue work, supported movement and stretching when appropriate, then reassess how movement feels.
Goal: full-body session
The New Routine provides the structural framework, while intake and client response determine what gets emphasized, shortened, modified or omitted.

Informed consent is ongoing

Before treatment
Discuss goals, regions to be treated, relevant draping, techniques or positioning the client may not expect, and reasonable alternatives.
During treatment
A yes at intake isn't permanent permission for every technique. Check in when the plan changes, when entering a sensitive region, or when client response suggests uncertainty.
Consent can be withdrawn
The client can ask to change pressure, skip a region, change position, stop a technique or end the session.
Power matters
Professional consent requires more than silence or lack of resistance. Don't pressure, shame or persuade a client into work they don't want.

Sensitive-area communication

Chest/pectoral, gluteal, abdominal, medial-thigh and other sensitive-region work requires especially clear communication, appropriate draping and adherence to law, scope, school policy and client boundaries. Explain what area you propose to work, why, how, and what alternatives exist. Consent to massage generally doesn't automatically equal consent to every sensitive-area technique.

SOAP documentation

S
Subjective
What the client reports: goals, symptoms, pain/tension description, relevant changes, activity, response to previous work. Example: “Client reports right upper-trapezius tightness after increased computer work.”
O
Objective
Relevant observations or assessment findings within massage scope. Example: “Right shoulder held slightly elevated; increased tissue resistance noted in right upper trapezius compared with left.” Avoid turning observations into medical diagnoses.
A
Assessment
Your massage-therapy interpretation of the session information and response. Example: “Focused upper-quarter work appropriate today; pressure tolerance decreased near right levator/upper trap.” Stay within scope.
P
Plan
What you did and/or what you plan next: regions, techniques, modifications, home-care suggestions within scope, follow-up and reassessment. Example: “Performed general Swedish routine with additional right upper-trap/pectoral work; modified pressure to client preference; reassess next visit.”

Write facts, not stories

Better
“Client requested that gluteal work be skipped today. Routine modified.”
Avoid
“Client was weird about glute work.” This is judgmental and clinically useless.
Better
“Client reported sharp pain with cervical rotation to the right. Neck stretch omitted and client advised to seek appropriate evaluation if symptoms persist/worsen.”
Avoid
“Client probably has a pinched nerve.” That's a diagnosis/speculation.
Better
“2-inch area of redness noted on left anterior shin; local massage avoided.”
Avoid
“Skin looked bad.” Be specific enough that the note has meaning later.

Turn the New Routine into a treatment plan

Case: A client books a full-body massage and reports their main goal is relaxation plus extra attention to neck/shoulder tension from desk work. They don't want abdominal work and prefer moderate pressure.

Structure
Use the New Routine as the full-session framework rather than inventing a new sequence.
Time
Keep the regional flow but protect extra minutes for upper back, shoulders and neck by slightly shortening lower-priority regions.
Pressure
Moderate is the starting preference, not a command to use identical force everywhere. Adjust by tissue and feedback.
Consent
Confirm the planned full-body regions and sensitive-area boundaries. Respect the client's decision to exclude abdominal work.
Reassess
Check pressure and response during focused shoulder/neck work, especially before stronger techniques or stretching.
Document
Record the goal, relevant findings, regions/techniques emphasized, excluded work, modifications and client response.

MBLEx + clinical reasoning

A client signed an intake form agreeing to massage but asks you to stop a technique during treatment. What takes priority?

The client's current consent. Stop or modify the technique. Consent is ongoing and can be withdrawn.

Which SOAP category contains information reported by the client?

Subjective.

“The client's shoulder pain is caused by cervical nerve compression.” Is that appropriate massage documentation?

No. It states a medical diagnosis/causal conclusion beyond massage scope. Document the client's report and relevant observations instead.

Why shouldn't every full-body massage use exactly the same time distribution?

Client goals, contraindications, tissue response, preferences and available session time differ. The routine supplies structure, while treatment planning supplies individualization.

What should happen if the client changes their mind about a region after treatment begins?

Respect the new boundary and modify the plan. Prior consent doesn't remove the client's right to change or withdraw consent.

What's the purpose of documentation?

To create an accurate professional record of relevant assessment information, treatment decisions, consent/modifications, work performed, client response and future planning.
