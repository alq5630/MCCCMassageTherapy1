Sports Massage

Sports massage changes with timing, athlete condition and performance demands. The massage an athlete receives before competition shouldn't look like the massage they receive during recovery.

Four contexts

Pre-event
Brief, stimulating and specific to readiness. Avoid creating soreness, fatigue or excessive relaxation.
Inter-event
Short recovery between bouts or events. Address immediate comfort while preserving readiness.
Post-event
Recovery-focused. Screen for acute injury, dehydration, heat illness and symptoms that need medical attention before massage.
Maintenance
Work between events can be more targeted and similar to a regular therapeutic session, while remaining within massage scope.

Pre-event toolbox

Use brisk rhythmic techniques, compression, shaking, active movement and sport-specific preparation when appropriate. Keep the work relatively short and familiar. Competition day isn't the time to introduce a painful technique or chase deep tissue changes.

Ask: What is the athlete about to do? Which tissues need to feel ready? How much time is available? Has the athlete used massage before competition?

Inter-event massage

Between events, the goal is usually to help the athlete feel recovered without making them sleepy, sore or temporarily unstable. Keep the session focused. Recheck hydration, temperature, cramping, pain and injury concerns before assuming every complaint needs massage.

Post-event priorities

First determine whether massage is appropriate. A suspected strain, sprain, fracture, concussion, exertional heat illness or other acute problem may require medical evaluation. When massage is appropriate, keep pressure and duration matched to the athlete's condition and recovery goals.

When not to “work it out”

Sudden severe pain, inability to bear weight, obvious deformity, rapidly increasing swelling, suspected concussion, altered mental status, chest pain, breathing difficulty, heat-illness signs or other serious symptoms aren't invitations for deeper massage. Stop and follow the appropriate emergency or referral pathway.

Maintenance work

Maintenance sessions can include broader therapeutic massage, mobility work, stretching, attention to repetitive loading patterns and recovery goals. Coordinate with the athlete's healthcare or training team when appropriate. Massage therapists can support recovery but don't independently diagnose or rehabilitate injuries outside scope.

Evidence-aware claims

Massage may help perceived soreness, relaxation, flexibility in some contexts and the athlete's short-term recovery experience. Don't promise that massage prevents all injuries, “flushes lactic acid,” dramatically speeds tissue healing or guarantees improved athletic performance.

Sports massage lab: same athlete, four sessions

Choose a sport and build four mini-treatment plans for the same athlete.

15 minutes before competition
Choose body regions, techniques, pace and what you'll avoid.
Between two events
You have 10 minutes. Decide what information you need before touching them.
Immediately after competition
Screen first. Decide what would make you refer rather than massage.
Three days later
Design a 50-minute maintenance session tied to the athlete's sport and goals.

Instructor check: The plan should change meaningfully with timing. If all four sessions look the same, rethink the clinical goal.

Quick scenarios

A runner asks for extremely deep calf work five minutes before a race. Best response?

Don't create pain or fatigue immediately before performance. Use brief familiar preparation techniques and save aggressive targeted work for an appropriate later session.

An athlete is confused, very hot and unsteady after an event. Massage?

No. Those findings can indicate a medical emergency such as exertional heat illness. Prioritize emergency response rather than massage.

What's wrong with saying massage “flushes lactic acid” after exercise?

It oversimplifies exercise physiology and overstates massage's mechanism. Describe realistic goals such as comfort, relaxation and perceived recovery instead.
