Myofascial Release

Learn fascia as connective tissue, then practice slow, sustained myofascial techniques as their own clinical tool rather than treating MFR as another name for trigger point work.

What is fascia?

Fascia is connective tissue that surrounds, supports, and links structures throughout the body. Its mechanical behavior is influenced by tissue composition, loading, movement, injury, inflammation, and the nervous system. In hands-on practice, therapists assess how superficial and deeper tissues glide and respond rather than assuming every painful area represents a discrete "fascial adhesion."

MFR is not trigger point therapy

Myofascial release and trigger point techniques can both appear in a targeted massage session, but they aren't the same technique. MFR emphasizes sustained tissue engagement and slow fascial stretch. Trigger point work focuses on localized hyperirritable/tender areas and their symptom patterns. Keep the concepts separate so technique selection stays intentional.

Foundational techniques

Skin rolling
Lift and roll superficial tissue to explore mobility and restriction.
Cross-hand stretch
Engage the tissue with hands apart and slowly take up slack in opposing directions.
Longitudinal glide
Move slowly along the tissue while maintaining deliberate engagement.
J-stroke
Use a small curved tissue-engagement motion where appropriate, including around fully healed scar tissue.

How it differs from Swedish and deep tissue

MFR is generally slower and uses little or no lubricant so the therapist can engage the tissue. The goal isn't maximum pressure. The therapist takes up tissue slack, waits, follows the response, and reassesses. Deep tissue refers to working with deeper layers and structures and can use multiple techniques. Neither label should be used as shorthand for "make it hurt."

Practical sequence

  1. Observe and palpate before treating.
  2. Choose a broad region related to the client's goal.
  3. Engage the tissue slowly without sliding over it.
  4. Hold or follow the tissue response rather than forcing movement.
  5. Ask about sensation and monitor guarding or breath holding.
  6. Reassess mobility, comfort, or the functional measure you started with.

Precautions

Avoid or modify MFR over acute infection or inflammation, open wounds, recent burns, unstable fractures or joints, suspected DVT, and tissues where pressure or stretch is medically inappropriate. Recent surgery and significant osteoporosis require additional caution and appropriate guidance.
