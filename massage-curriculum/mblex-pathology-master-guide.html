Pathology, Cautions & Massage Decisions

The licensing exam does not require a massage therapist to diagnose disease. It does require you to recognize common pathology concepts, identify contraindications and areas of caution, account for special populations and medications, and choose the safest entry-level response.

The question behind almost every pathology question

What changes about the massage? Sort the situation into one of four decisions: postpone the whole session, avoid a local area, modify the technique/session, or gather more information before proceeding. Emergency or red-flag findings shift the priority away from massage and toward appropriate medical/emergency care.

Pathology language

Pathology
The study of disease and the structural/functional changes associated with disease.
Etiology
The cause or origin of a disease or disorder. Idiopathic means the cause is unknown.
Signs vs symptoms
Signs are observable/measurable findings. Symptoms are experiences reported by the client.
Acute vs chronic
Acute generally describes rapid onset/short duration; chronic describes persistent or long-lasting conditions. Do not assume chronic means harmless.
Local vs systemic
Local affects a limited region. Systemic involves the body more broadly or multiple systems.
Remission / exacerbation
Some chronic and autoimmune conditions cycle between quieter periods and symptom flares.

High-yield musculoskeletal conditions

ConditionWhat it is / cluesMassage reasoning
StrainInjury to muscle or tendon tissue. Pain may increase with resisted contraction or stretch.Acute, inflamed or significantly injured tissue calls for local avoidance/modification and possibly medical evaluation. Later-stage work depends on healing status and tolerance.
SprainInjury to a ligament supporting a joint.Do not aggressively stress an acutely injured or unstable joint. Respect swelling, pain, instability and medical restrictions.
OsteoarthritisDegenerative joint disease involving cartilage and other joint tissues, often with pain/stiffness.Comfortable massage to surrounding tissues and gentle movement may be appropriate when tolerated. Modify during acute irritation and for limited ROM.
Rheumatoid arthritisSystemic autoimmune inflammatory disease that commonly affects synovial joints.During active flare, avoid vigorous work on inflamed joints and modify the session. Between flares, comfortable work may support relaxation and symptom management. Consider medications and systemic involvement.
OsteoporosisReduced bone strength and increased fracture risk.Avoid aggressive pressure, forceful mobilization and positions that create unnecessary fracture risk. Severity and fracture history matter.
BursitisInflammation/irritation of a bursa.Avoid compressing an acutely inflamed bursa. Work elsewhere or modify based on symptoms.
TendinopathyUmbrella term for tendon pain/injury; may involve acute inflammation or chronic degenerative change.Do not treat every tendon complaint as “tendinitis.” Acute inflammatory presentation requires gentler/local modification; chronic presentations require individualized assessment.
Plantar heel/fascial painPain involving plantar structures, often near the calcaneal attachment, with multiple possible contributors.Work within tolerance and consider calf/foot mechanics. Do not promise to “break up” fascia or diagnose the cause of heel pain.
Disc disorderDisc degeneration, bulging or herniation may be symptomatic or asymptomatic and can involve nerve irritation.Position for comfort. Avoid movements/pressure that reproduce neurological symptoms. New weakness, bowel/bladder changes, saddle sensory change or severe progressive neurological findings require medical attention.
Joint replacementProsthetic replacement of joint surfaces.Recent surgery requires surgical restrictions and healing precautions. With older replacements, respect ROM limits, positioning and client/provider instructions.

Nervous system conditions

Peripheral neuropathy
Peripheral nerve dysfunction can cause numbness, tingling, pain or weakness. Reduced sensation makes client pressure feedback less reliable, so use caution and avoid extremes of pressure or temperature.
Carpal tunnel syndrome
Median nerve compression at the wrist can produce sensory symptoms in its distribution and motor changes. Do not reproduce symptoms; treat it as a condition requiring careful regional assessment rather than assuming every hand symptom is CTS.
Multiple sclerosis
A CNS demyelinating disease with variable neurological symptoms. Fatigue, altered sensation, weakness, spasticity and heat sensitivity can affect session planning.
Headache
Tension-type headaches may coexist with muscular tension, but headache plus neurological deficits, severe sudden onset, fever/confusion, major trauma or other red flags is not a routine massage presentation.

Cardiovascular and vascular cautions

Hypertension
High blood pressure may be asymptomatic. Consider overall stability, cardiovascular complications and medication effects such as dizziness. Do not use an old single cutoff as a universal massage rule.
Varicose veins
Dilated superficial veins. Avoid direct heavy pressure over prominent/problematic veins, especially with skin/tissue changes. Do not confuse varicosities with DVT.
Suspected DVT
Potential deep-vein thrombosis is a serious contraindication to massage of the affected region and requires urgent medical evaluation. Concerning unilateral swelling, warmth, pain or unexplained color change should not be “massaged out.”
Raynaud phenomenon
Episodic vasospasm commonly affecting digits. Maintain comfortable temperature, protect skin and account for medications and sensory changes.

Skin and infectious conditions

Psoriasis
Chronic immune-mediated skin disease and not contagious. Avoid open, cracked, bleeding or acutely irritated areas; intact comfortable skin may be massaged.
Fungal/bacterial/viral skin infection
Potentially transmissible lesions require infection-control reasoning and usually local avoidance or postponement depending on extent, contagiousness and systemic symptoms.
Open wound
A portal of entry and vulnerable tissue. Do not massage directly over an open wound.
Fever/systemic illness
Systemic signs suggest the client may not be appropriate for routine massage at that time. Use current infection-control guidance rather than memorizing one universal “fever-free X hours” rule.

Respiratory, digestive and endocrine examples

Sinus inflammation
May be infectious or noninfectious. Fever/systemic illness changes the decision. Positioning also matters because lying flat may worsen symptoms.
GERD
Reflux of gastric contents can irritate the esophagus. Avoid positions or abdominal pressure that worsen symptoms; timing after meals and elevation may improve comfort.
Diabetes mellitus
A metabolic disease involving impaired glucose regulation. Consider medication/insulin timing, hypoglycemia awareness, neuropathy, circulation, skin integrity and wound healing.
Thyroid disorders
Can influence metabolism, energy, temperature tolerance and cardiovascular function. Massage decisions depend on symptoms, stability and associated treatment rather than the diagnosis name alone.

Red flags: massage is no longer the priority

Examples include sudden chest pain, severe shortness of breath, signs of stroke, fainting with concerning symptoms, severe allergic reaction, uncontrolled bleeding, major trauma, sudden severe headache with neurological signs, or signs concerning for DVT. Stay within scope: recognize the concern, stop/postpone massage and obtain appropriate emergency or medical help.

Medication classes: know what changes

Class/exampleMassage concern to think about
Anticoagulant / antiplateletIncreased bleeding/bruising risk. Pressure and techniques may need modification.
AntihypertensiveDizziness or orthostatic symptoms may occur. Allow slow position changes and monitor comfort.
Analgesic / anti-inflammatoryPain may be masked. Do not use reduced pain perception as permission for excessive pressure.
Muscle relaxant / sedating medicationDrowsiness, dizziness and altered feedback may affect positioning, transfers and pressure choices.
Diabetes medication / insulinBe aware of hypoglycemia risk and session timing; clients should follow their medical management plan.
CorticosteroidEffects vary by route/duration and can include tissue/skin changes, immune effects and bone concerns. Gather relevant information rather than applying one blanket rule.

MBLEx decision shortcut

When several answers sound plausible, favor the answer that protects the client, respects scope, gathers necessary information, obtains consent and modifies appropriately. The massage therapist does not diagnose, prescribe, promise cures or ignore red flags.

Practice reasoning

A client has a visibly swollen, warm, painful calf on one side with no clear explanation. Deep calf massage or referral?

Do not massage the area. The presentation can be concerning for a vascular problem such as DVT and warrants prompt medical evaluation.

A client with stable psoriasis has intact plaques that are not painful or cracked. Is psoriasis itself contagious?

No. Psoriasis is not contagious. Modify or avoid irritated/open areas based on tissue condition and client comfort.

A client taking an anticoagulant asks for very deep work because “deep is the only thing that works.” What matters most?

Medication-related bruising/bleeding risk and safe pressure selection take priority over the client's request for intensity. Discuss and modify the plan.

A client with peripheral neuropathy cannot reliably feel pressure in the feet. What changes?

Use extra caution with pressure and temperature because normal sensory feedback is impaired. Inspect tissue, communicate carefully and avoid relying on “tell me if it is too much” as the only safeguard.

During intake a client reports a diagnosis you do not understand. Best next step?

Gather more information before proceeding. Stay within scope and do not guess at safety based only on the condition's name.
