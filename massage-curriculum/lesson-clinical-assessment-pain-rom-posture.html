Clinical Reasoning · Assessment

Pain, Range of Motion & Posture

Assessment isn't diagnosis. It's a structured way to gather useful information before and after massage so your treatment decisions are safer, more specific and easier to evaluate.

What assessment is for

Establish a baseline
What does the client report and what can they comfortably do before treatment?
Guide treatment
Assessment helps determine which regions deserve attention and which techniques or positions may need modification.
Identify precautions
Swelling, redness, heat, rash, painful movement or unexpected neurological symptoms can change the treatment plan.
Reassess
Repeat a relevant measure after treatment. You're looking for change, not trying to prove that your massage “fixed” a diagnosis.

Pain is more than tissue damage

Pain is a sensory and emotional experience. Nociceptive information matters, but pain can also be influenced by previous experiences, beliefs, emotions, context, sleep, stress and nervous-system processing. This is why two people with apparently similar tissue situations can experience very different pain.

Nociception
Neural signaling related to actual or potential tissue threat. Nociception and the conscious experience of pain aren't identical.
Nociceptive pain
Pain associated with activation of nociceptors. Acute injury and inflammation are common contexts.
Neuropathic pain
Pain associated with lesion/disease or dysfunction involving neural structures. Burning, electric, shooting or tingling descriptions may make neural involvement worth considering without diagnosing it.
Sensitization
Nervous-system responsiveness can increase, lowering the threshold at which stimuli become painful. More pressure isn't automatically the answer to greater sensitivity.

Ask better pain questions

Location
“Show me where you feel it.” Ask whether it stays local or travels.
Quality
Aching, burning, sharp, dull, throbbing, tingling, electric, tight, tender or another description?
Timing
When did it begin? Constant or intermittent? Does time of day matter?
Behavior
What makes it better or worse? Movement, position, activity, rest, pressure?
Intensity
A rating can help track one person's experience over time, but don't treat a number as an objective measurement of tissue damage.
Function
What can the client not do, or what has become difficult? Function often gives treatment planning more meaning than a pain number alone.

Range of motion

AROM · Active
The client moves the joint using their own muscles. Observe available range, quality, symptom response and compensations.
PROM · Passive
An external force moves the relaxed joint. Compare with active motion when appropriate and pay attention to comfort and end feel.
Resisted movement
The client contracts against resistance without necessarily moving through full range. This can provide information about contractile tissue response when used within training and scope.
End feel
The quality perceived at the end of passive range. Students should learn normal concepts without forcing a joint just to “find” an end feel.

Simple ROM reasoning

Active hurts, passive easier
Contractile activity may be contributing. This is information for reasoning, not proof of a specific lesion.
Both active and passive limited
Joint, connective-tissue, pain-protection or other factors may contribute. Don't jump from a pattern to a diagnosis.
Unexpected weakness
Stop treating strength as merely “tight muscles.” Significant or new weakness can require referral depending on context.
Symptoms travel
Radiating pain, numbness or tingling changes the clinical picture. Document it and stay within scope.

Posture without the posture police

Posture assessment describes what you see. It shouldn't become a hunt for imperfections or a promise that every asymmetry causes pain. People naturally vary, and posture changes with task, mood, fatigue, habit and environment.

Anterior view
Observe general head position, shoulder height, trunk alignment, pelvic landmarks when appropriate, knees and feet.
Lateral view
Observe head position, spinal curves, shoulder relationship, pelvis, knees and ankle relationship without demanding a single “perfect” plumb line.
Posterior view
Observe head/neck, scapulae, spinal/trunk symmetry, pelvis, lower limbs and feet.
Meaning
An observation becomes useful when connected with the client's symptoms, function, movement and goals. A visual asymmetry by itself isn't a diagnosis.

Assessment boundaries

Obtain consent before physical assessment and explain what you're doing. Compare sides when useful, generally observe the nonaffected side before the affected side, and keep the process relaxed. Assessments can make clients feel watched or self-conscious.

Massage assessments help you understand what may be happening and determine treatment safety. They don't authorize a massage therapist to diagnose orthopedic, neurological or medical conditions.

Connect assessment to the New Routine

Neck Stretch
Check comfortable cervical movement before deciding how much traction or stretch belongs in today's routine. Reassess afterward if neck mobility was a treatment goal.
Arm Extend
Shoulder ROM and client response determine the available movement. The named routine step doesn't create permission to force range.
Quad / hamstring work
If the client's goal involves hip or knee movement, simple pre/post ROM can help students connect tissue work with function.
Jaw Ease
Ask about jaw symptoms and observe comfortable opening/closing before focused work rather than assuming masseter tension from palpation alone.
Pressure selection
High pain sensitivity or sensitization means “deeper” may be counterproductive. Client response guides dosage.
Documentation
Record relevant baseline findings and meaningful post-treatment changes without claiming a cure or diagnosis.

Assessment lab

Ask a partner about one pain/tension complaint using location, quality, timing, behavior, intensity and function.
Choose one joint and observe active ROM bilaterally.
With consent and within class instruction, compare appropriate passive ROM.
Describe posture from one view using neutral observational language only.
Create one treatment-plan modification from the assessment findings.
Perform the relevant portion of the New Routine.
Reassess one useful measure and document the change without diagnosing.

MBLEx + clinical reasoning

What's the difference between active and passive ROM?

Active ROM is produced by the client's voluntary muscle contraction. Passive ROM is produced by an external force while the client remains relaxed.

Does more pain necessarily mean more tissue damage?

No. Pain is multidimensional and influenced by nervous-system processing, context, emotion, experience and other factors as well as tissue state.

Why reassess after treatment?

It gives you information about how the client responded and whether a relevant symptom or functional measure changed, helping guide future treatment.

A student sees one shoulder higher than the other. Can they conclude that this is causing the client's pain?

No. They can document the observation and compare it with symptoms, movement and other information, but the asymmetry alone doesn't establish causation.

What's an appropriate response to new significant weakness or neurological symptoms?

Stop treating it as a routine muscle-tension problem, remain within scope and recommend appropriate medical evaluation based on the situation.

Why do assessments require consent?

They involve observation, touch and sometimes movement or positioning beyond ordinary massage contact. The client should understand what will happen and why.
