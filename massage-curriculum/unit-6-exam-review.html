Theory Exam #6 Review

Review business structures, worker classification, insurance, money, professional boundaries and safe spa-service decision making.

Fast distinctions

W-2
Employee wage statement.
1099-NEC
Common reporting form for nonemployee compensation.
Professional liability
Claims involving professional services or alleged negligence.
General liability
Many premises and ordinary business risks.
Product liability
Claims involving products sold, supplied or used.
Scope of practice
Legal and professional limits on what a therapist may do.
LLC
State-law legal entity. Tax treatment is a separate issue.
S corporation
Federal tax election rather than a standalone state business type.

Worker classification

Look at the actual relationship, not just the contract title. Consider control over schedule, prices, methods and procedures; who provides equipment; financial risk; independence; and opportunity for profit or loss. A business can't automatically turn an employee into an independent contractor by handing them a 1099.

Money concepts

  • Revenue: money the business earns before expenses.
  • Expenses: costs of operating the business.
  • Profit: revenue minus deductible business expenses in the simplified accounting sense.
  • Break-even: the point where revenue covers costs.
  • Cash reserve: money intentionally retained to absorb fluctuations and unexpected costs.

Self-employed therapists need systems for taxes and recordkeeping. Don't assume every dollar in the business account is spendable income.

Clinical professionalism still applies in spa services

Hot stones, heat, cold and body scrubs require screening, consent, sanitation and ongoing monitoring. A client asking for something doesn't erase a contraindication. Products, temperatures and tools create additional variables the therapist must control.

Exam-style cases

  1. A client slips on a loose rug while walking to the treatment room. Which type of coverage is most directly relevant?
  2. A client alleges that negligent massage technique caused an injury. Which coverage is most directly relevant?
  3. A clinic calls therapists independent contractors but dictates their schedules, prices, uniforms and treatment procedures. What's the concern?
  4. A therapist advertises that massage “cures rheumatoid arthritis.” Identify the professional problem.
  5. A self-employed therapist collects $6,000 this month. Why isn't $6,000 automatically their take-home pay?
  6. A client with reduced heat sensation asks for very hot stones because they “can barely feel them.” What's the safest reasoning?
  7. A therapist reaches into a bulk scrub container with product-covered hands during a service. What's wrong with this?
  8. A practice's business plan requires 45 hands-on sessions every week to pay its bills. What sustainability question should the owner ask?

Check your reasoning

1. Loose rugGeneral liability is the most directly relevant category because this is a premises-type incident.
2. Alleged treatment injuryProfessional liability is the most directly relevant category.
3. Contractor with heavy clinic controlThe real relationship may resemble employment. Classification depends on applicable legal tests, not simply the label used by the clinic.
4. “Cures rheumatoid arthritis”It makes an inappropriate medical claim and exceeds what a massage therapist should promise within professional scope.
5. $6,000 collectedRevenue must still cover business expenses, tax obligations and other financial commitments before determining owner income.
6. Reduced heat sensationImpaired ability to perceive temperature increases burn risk. More heat isn't the answer simply because the client can't feel it normally.
7. Bulk scrub contaminationUsed hands can contaminate the bulk product. Portion product before treatment and follow infection-control procedures.
8. Forty-five sessions every weekAsk whether that volume is physically and operationally sustainable and whether the pricing or expense structure needs revision.
