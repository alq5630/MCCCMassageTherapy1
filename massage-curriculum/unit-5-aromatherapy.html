Aromatherapy Practicum

Use essential oils as an optional sensory component of massage with informed consent, conservative dilution, careful screening, and claims that stay inside massage therapy scope.

Essential oils aren't automatically safe because they're natural

Concentrated plant extracts can irritate skin, trigger sensitization, worsen respiratory symptoms, interact with health conditions, and create unwanted exposure for clients or therapists. Aromatherapy should always be optional. A client who doesn't want fragrance gets a fragrance-free session.

Where essential oils come from

Flowers
Examples include lavender, rose, geranium, and chamomile.
Leaves & needles
Examples include eucalyptus, peppermint, rosemary, and conifers.
Citrus peels
Lemon, lime, orange, grapefruit, and bergamot oils are commonly expressed from the peel.
Wood, bark, roots & resins
Cedarwood, sandalwood, cinnamon bark, vetiver, frankincense, and myrrh are examples.

How aroma is perceived

Volatile molecules reach receptors in the olfactory epithelium, and olfactory signals connect with brain regions involved in perception, memory, emotion, and behavior. Smell can strongly influence a person's experience of a massage without requiring claims that an oil "detoxes" the body, cures disease, or changes a person's biological frequency.

Dilution

For topical massage, essential oils are generally diluted into a carrier oil or other appropriate unscented base. Use conservative concentrations and follow evidence-based safety guidance for the specific oil, client, body area, age, pregnancy status, and health history. More drops aren't automatically more therapeutic.

Safety screening

Skin
Avoid broken, irritated, infected, or reactive skin. Stop if burning, itching, redness, or other irritation develops.
Respiratory sensitivity
Strong scents can be problematic for asthma, migraine, fragrance sensitivity, nausea, and other conditions.
Photosensitivity
Some expressed citrus oils can increase UV sensitivity. Know the specific oil and product before topical use.
Pregnancy, children & complex health histories
Use additional caution and oil-specific safety information rather than blanket lists of "safe" oils.

Practicum: build a massage blend

  1. Ask the client whether they want aromatherapy at all.
  2. Screen health history, allergies, fragrance sensitivity, pregnancy, medications, and skin condition.
  3. Select one or more oils based on aroma preference and safety, not disease-treatment promises.
  4. Dilute into a labeled carrier base using a conservative concentration.
  5. Let the client smell the blend before applying it broadly.
  6. Document the oils/products used and any response.

Professional claims

You can describe an oil's aroma and say that a client may find a scent relaxing, refreshing, grounding, or pleasant. Don't tell clients an essential oil treats cancer, eliminates toxins, cures infection, replaces medication, or has another medical effect you can't support and aren't licensed to treat.
