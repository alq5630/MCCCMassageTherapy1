Swedish Massage Strokes

The six foundational technique families are only the beginning. Learn how each is performed, how its variations differ, and how pressure, rate, rhythm, direction and tissue response change the technique.

E-P-F-C-T-V

E

Effleurage

Gliding movements that follow the contours of the body. After lubricant is applied, the hands or forearms travel over the skin while maintaining smooth contact. Deeper effleurage should come from efficient body-weight transfer rather than muscular pushing.

one-handedtwo-handedalternate handthumb/fingernerve stroke

One-handed: one primary contact surface applies the stroke. The opposite hand may reinforce it when appropriate. Two-handed: both hands work together. Alternate-hand: one hand follows the other to create continuous contact.

Nerve stroke / feathering: very light effleurage commonly used to finish a body region or session. It may be performed on bare skin, over a drape or over clothing.

P

Petrissage

Compression, lifting or decompression, and release of soft tissue. The therapist grasps tissue, lifts it when appropriate, compresses it and releases while maintaining controlled contact.

one-handedtwo-handedalternate handskin rolling

Superficial petrissage primarily engages skin and superficial tissue. Deeper petrissage engages a muscle or muscle group more fully. Avoid trying to physically lift the client's body from the table. Tissue manipulation should never require unsafe lifting mechanics.

Skin rolling: lift a fold of relatively unlubricated or lightly lubricated skin and advance it across the region with a controlled rolling action.

F

Friction

Rubbing or shearing one surface against another. Superficial friction allows the therapist's contact to move over the skin. With deep friction, the therapist's hand and the client's skin move together over deeper tissue.

warmingrollingwringingtransversechuckingcircular

Warming friction: brisk superficial movement used to generate warmth. Rolling friction: compress tissue while the hands move back and forth. Wringing: hands move in opposing directions.

Transverse / cross-fiber friction: the skin and contact move perpendicular to the tissue fibers. Chucking: movement is parallel to the fibers. Circular friction: skin moves in small circles over the target area.

C

Compression

A non-gliding pressure technique that can be sustained or rhythmic and can be performed on bare skin, through a drape or through clothing.

sustainedrhythmicone-handedtwo-handedalternate hand

Sustained compression: gradually apply pressure toward the underlying tissue using an appropriate contact such as fingers, thumb, palm, fist, forearm or elbow. Rhythmic compression: repeatedly press and release in a regular pattern.

Two-handed compression uses both hands at the same time. Alternate-hand compression uses the hands at different times to create rhythmic pressure.

T

Tapotement

Repetitive percussion in which the contact strikes and leaves the tissue quickly. Intensity changes with speed, force, contact surface and the body region being treated.

tappingpincementhackingcuppingpoundingclappingdiffused

Tapping: fingertips strike lightly or briskly. A very light fingertip variation is sometimes called raindrops. Pincement: fingertips and thumb rapidly strike, grasp, lift and release superficial tissue.

Hacking: relaxed ulnar edges of the hands alternate or strike together. Keep wrists flexible. Cupping: cupped hands trap and release air, producing the characteristic hollow sound.

Pounding: sides of loose fists strike larger muscular regions. A knuckle-based loose-fist variation is sometimes called rapping. Clapping: open palms strike rhythmically. Diffused tapotement: one relaxed hand rests on the client while the other hand strikes the back of it, dispersing force.

V

Vibration

Shaking or trembling movements that can be rapid or slow and applied with fingers, hands or appropriate mechanical equipment.

fine vibrationjostlingfluffing

Fine vibration: a small trembling motion transferred into tissue. The contact can stay in one place or move. Jostling: grasp a muscle group, joint region or extremity and create a larger shaking motion while keeping the movement controlled.

Fluffing: a gentle lift-and-release variation that may include light traction of an extremity. Traction must remain within the therapist's scope and the client's comfortable, available range.

Technique characteristics matter

Pressure
Light, medium or deep describes force and tissue displacement. More pressure isn't automatically more effective.
Direction
Traditional Swedish instruction often emphasizes centripetal movement on extremities. Friction and compression don't depend on the same long gliding direction.
Excursion
The distance traveled by the stroke. It may be a few centimeters or span a large body region.
Rate & rhythm
Rate is speed or repetitions. Rhythm is regularity. Slower work often feels more calming while faster work is often more stimulating.
MBLEx thinking: identify the technique by what the tissue and therapist's contact are actually doing. Gliding = effleurage. Lift/knead/release = petrissage. Shearing/rubbing = friction. Non-gliding pressure = compression. Percussion = tapotement. Trembling/shaking = vibration.

Technique safety

Don't perform a textbook movement just because it has a name. Modify or avoid techniques when the client's tissue condition, health status, positioning, pain response or contraindications make the movement inappropriate.

Some older technique descriptions involve lifting or moving substantial portions of the client's body. The therapist must consider the load, table height, distance of the load from the spine, twisting angle and their own physical capacity. A technique isn't worth injuring yourself to perform.

Technique lab

Demonstrate all six E-P-F-C-T-V families and identify the defining mechanical action of each.
Perform one-handed, two-handed and alternate-hand effleurage.
Practice superficial and deeper petrissage without attempting to lift the client's body weight.
Compare superficial warming friction with transverse, chucking and circular deep friction.
Demonstrate sustained and rhythmic compression through both bare skin and a drape.
Practice tapping, hacking, cupping, pounding and diffused tapotement with relaxed wrists and controlled force.
Compare fine vibration with larger jostling.
Change rate and pressure while your partner describes how the treatment quality changes.

MBLEx-style check

A therapist's hand and the client's skin move together perpendicular to a tendon or muscle fibers. Which technique is this?

Transverse, or cross-fiber, friction.

Which technique is defined by lifting, compressing and releasing soft tissue?

Petrissage.

What's the key difference between effleurage and compression?

Effleurage is a gliding technique. Compression applies pressure without gliding.

A therapist uses alternating relaxed ulnar edges of the hands in rapid percussion. What is it?

Hacking tapotement.

What distinguishes chucking friction from transverse friction?

Chucking moves parallel to the tissue fibers. Transverse friction moves perpendicular to them.

A technique creates a trembling movement without percussion. Which family is it?

Vibration.
