Swedish Massage Strokes

Learn the foundational technique families, then connect each technique to treatment goals, client response, body mechanics, precautions, and clinical decision making.

E-P-F-C-T-V

E

Effleurage

Gliding or stroking movements that follow body contours. Effleurage can introduce touch and pressure, distribute lubricant, assess tissue, connect techniques, and begin or finish work on a region.

one-handedtwo-handedalternate handlight/nerve stroke
P

Petrissage

Lifting, kneading, compressing, rolling, and releasing soft tissue. Adjust the amount of tissue engaged and the force used to the client's comfort and tissue condition.

kneadingliftingwringingskin rolling
F

Friction

Rubbing or shearing techniques that may be superficial or more focused. Friction is not appropriate over every tissue state. Acute inflammation, fragile tissue, skin irritation, and other local contraindications may require avoidance or modification.

warmingcircularparallelcross-fiber
C

Compression

Pressure applied without gliding. It can be broad or focused, sustained or rhythmic. Broad contact generally distributes force more comfortably than a small contact surface.

sustainedrhythmicpalmarforearm
T

Tapotement

Rhythmic percussion performed with relaxed, controlled hands. Faster percussion is often used when a more stimulating treatment quality is desired, but technique selection must still fit the client and region.

hackingcuppingclappingtappingpounding
V

Vibration

Rapid trembling, shaking, or oscillating movement transferred into the tissues. Fine vibration is smaller and more localized; shaking or jostling creates larger movement.

fine vibrationshakingjostling

MBLEx thinking: technique follows the goal

Introduce & assess
Broad effleurage and compression can establish contact and help the therapist gather tactile information.
Engage soft tissue
Petrissage, compression, friction, and other techniques may be selected based on tissue state and treatment goal.
Stimulate
Brisker pacing, tapotement, shaking, or other rhythmic techniques may create a more stimulating experience.
Integrate & finish
Broader, smoother techniques can reconnect an area after focused work and provide an opportunity to reassess response.

The exam is more useful when you think beyond “What is this stroke called?” Ask: Why would I choose it, when would I modify it, and what client response tells me to continue or change?

Direction and circulation claims

Massage education traditionally emphasizes centripetal strokes on the extremities, meaning movement toward the trunk. Know the term and the traditional rationale, but don't turn it into an exaggerated claim that massage forcefully “pushes toxins” or manually controls circulation in a healthy person. Technique pressure and direction should be appropriate to anatomy, tissue condition, client comfort, and the intended treatment effect.

Technique lab

Demonstrate each E-P-F-C-T-V family and name it while performing it.
Change pressure, rate, rhythm, and contact surface without losing safe body mechanics.
Compare broad compression with a smaller contact surface and describe how force distribution changes.
Choose one technique for relaxation and one for a more stimulating treatment quality, then explain your reasoning.
Given a locally irritated or acutely inflamed area, identify which techniques you'd avoid or modify.
After focused work, return to a broad technique and reassess tissue and client feedback.

MBLEx-style check

A therapist wants to introduce touch, distribute lubricant, and assess a broad region. Which technique is the best starting choice?

Effleurage. It provides broad gliding contact and can accomplish all three goals.

Which technique family primarily lifts, kneads, and compresses soft tissue?

Petrissage.

A client has acute inflammation in a small area. Should the therapist apply aggressive friction directly over it?

No. Acute inflammation is a reason to avoid or modify direct local work.

What is the difference between rate and rhythm?

Rate is speed or repetition frequency. Rhythm is the regularity or pattern of the movement.

Why is “deep” not automatically the best pressure?

Pressure must fit the client's goals, tissue condition, comfort, contraindications, and response. Greater force isn't inherently more therapeutic.
